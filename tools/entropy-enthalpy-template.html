SECTION 2.4 · FROM CHEMISTRY 12 TO PHYSICAL CHEMISTRY

Entropy versus
enthalpy.

Why do some reactions release heat yet resist going forward, while others absorb heat and happen spontaneously? The answer comes from applying energy conservation to the whole entropy balance.

The question behind the textbook’s two tendencies

Does nature seek lower enthalpy, higher entropy, or something that combines both?

\[\boxed{\Delta G=\Delta H-T\Delta S=-T\Delta S_{\mathrm{universe}}}\]

For a closed system between states at the same temperature, at constant pressure, with a thermal reservoir at that temperature and only pressure–volume work. Section 06 derives each condition.

Sections 01–10 build the core explanation. The two starting points in section 03 distinguish constructing S from T and obtaining T from S. Sections 11–16 extend the explanation to chemical potentials, equilibrium, heat capacities, molecular probabilities and kinetics. Open the worked checks as you go. Equations, examples and the explorer work offline.

01What actually decides direction?

The universal criterion is an increase in the total entropy of an isolated whole. Lowering enthalpy becomes useful because heat released by the system can increase the entropy of its surroundings.

Choose the reacting material as the system. Its environment is the surroundings. “Universe” here means the isolated combination needed for the entropy accounting, not a claim about cosmology. A closed system exchanges energy but no matter; an isolated system exchanges neither.

First lawWhere does the energy go?
Second lawWhich direction increases total entropy?
Gibbs energyExpress that criterion using system properties at fixed T and P.

A system can become more ordered as it crystallizes. This is compatible with the second law if the released heat increases the surroundings’ entropy by a greater amount. Conversely, a system can gain entropy while its process remains unfavourable if it removes too much entropy from the surroundings.

What “spontaneous” means. A thermodynamically favourable direction is available under the stated constraints without a compensating external drive. It does not specify a reaction rate or guarantee an observable change within a short time.

02The first law: why enthalpy appears

Use the chemistry sign convention: heat entering the system is positive, and work done on the system is positive. Energy conservation gives:

\[\Delta U=q+w,\qquad \delta w_{PV}=-P_{\mathrm{ext}}\,dV\]

Expansion does work on the surroundings, so its work contribution is negative. Heat and work are transfers along a path; a system does not contain “heat.” Internal energy is a state function, so its change depends only on the endpoints.

Define enthalpy by adding the pressure–volume term:

\[H=U+PV\]
\[\begin{aligned}\Delta U&=q_P-P\Delta V,\\\Delta H&=\Delta U+P\Delta V=q_P.\end{aligned}\]

This finite-change derivation assumes constant external pressure, initial and final mechanical equilibrium at that pressure, and only PV work. It does not require a reversible chemical reaction. The result explains why calorimetry at constant pressure measures an enthalpy change.

Exothermic: ΔH < 0

The system loses enthalpy and releases heat under these conditions. The surroundings gain energy.

Endothermic: ΔH > 0

The system gains enthalpy and absorbs heat. The surroundings supply the energy.

The first law permits either direction as long as energy balances. It cannot by itself explain why heat flows from a hot object to a cold one or why gases mix.

Enthalpy is more than a bond-energy total

Breaking bonds requires energy; forming bonds releases it. But a reaction enthalpy also reflects intermolecular interactions, phases, solvation and the PV term. Average gas-phase bond energies give estimates, not a universal replacement for thermochemical data.

\[\Delta_rH^\circ=\sum_i\nu_i\Delta_fH_i^\circ\]

Here the stoichiometric number ν is positive for a product and negative for a reactant. Formation enthalpies of elements in their reference states are zero by convention; their standard molar entropies at room temperature are generally not zero.

03The second law: what entropy measures macroscopically

Two starting points: temperature first, or entropy first

There are two useful logical routes to the same equilibrium thermodynamics. In the macroscopic heat-engine construction, temperature comes first and entropy is then constructed from reversible heat. In the statistical construction, state counting supplies entropy first and its energy dependence determines temperature.

RouteWhat is established first?What follows?
Classical thermodynamics: T firstThe zeroth law establishes empirical temperature through thermal equilibrium. Reversible-engine heat ratios establish an absolute Kelvin scale using the second law.Using that T, Clausius’ equality proves that the reversible-heat integral is path-independent. This constructs the entropy state function.
Statistical mechanics: S firstA microscopic model supplies the accessible-state count as a function of energy, volume and particle numbers. Boltzmann’s formula assigns entropy to those macrostates.Maximizing the combined state count identifies an equilibrium energy derivative. Its reciprocal gives statistical temperature, which is then matched to the Kelvin scale.

Route A · Temperature and reversible heat determine entropy differences

\[T,\ \delta q_{\mathrm{rev}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad S(B)-S(A)=\int_A^B\frac{\delta q_{\mathrm{rev}}}{T}\]

Route B · The energy dependence of microscopic entropy determines temperature

\[\Omega(U,V,\{N_i\})\quad\Longrightarrow\quad S_B=k_B\ln\Omega\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac1T=\left(\frac{\partial S_B}{\partial U}\right)_{V,\{N_i\}}\]

What information is needed? The first route needs heat-transfer data as well as T to calculate entropy differences. The second needs the function S(U,V,{Ni}), including how it varies with U at fixed volume and species amounts; a single entropy value cannot determine temperature.

Once the two constructions are connected, both satisfy the same reversible relation. At fixed volume, fixed species amounts and with no other work modes:

\[\delta q_{\mathrm{rev}}=dU,\qquad dS=\frac{dU}{T}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\frac1T=\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial U}\right)_{V,\{N_i\}}\]

On the classical route the derivative identity is a consequence. On the statistical route it identifies temperature after entropy has been specified. The equivalence requires the temperature calibration and equilibrium assumptions explained in section 04. Keeping these starting assumptions separate prevents a circular argument.

Follow route A: construct entropy from reversible heat

Clausius defines entropy using reversible heat divided by absolute temperature. The second law explains why this particular combination is a state-function change:

\[dS=\frac{\delta q_{\mathrm{rev}}}{T},\qquad \Delta S=\int_1^2\frac{\delta q_{\mathrm{rev}}}{T}\]

Where does dS = δqrev/T come from?

The logical order matters. We first establish a result about reversible heat engines without assuming entropy exists. We then prove that the integral of δqrev/T depends only on the endpoints, and define that state-function difference as entropy. Energy conservation alone cannot establish this result.

Step 1 · A reversible engine relates heat to absolute temperature

A heat engine completes a cycle: its working material returns to its starting state, taking heat from a hot reservoir, delivering some work, and rejecting heat to a cold reservoir. Let qh > 0 be heat entering the engine at Th, and qc < 0 be heat entering it at Tc (negative because this heat leaves). Since the engine returns to the same internal energy, its net work output is qh + qc.

Carnot’s theorem, a consequence of the second law, says that all reversible engines between the same two reservoirs have the same efficiency. If they did not, operating one forward and another backward could transfer heat from cold to hot without a compensating work input. On the absolute thermodynamic temperature scale, their efficiency is:

\[\eta_{\mathrm{rev}}=\frac{W_{\mathrm{out}}}{q_h}=1+\frac{q_c}{q_h}=1-\frac{T_c}{T_h}\]

Rearrange. The two heat exchanges, divided by their respective reservoir temperatures, cancel:

\[\frac{q_c}{q_h}=-\frac{T_c}{T_h}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{q_h}{T_h}+\frac{q_c}{T_c}=0\]

The Carnot cycle has two isothermal heat-transfer steps and two adiabatic steps with zero heat transfer. Therefore this sum is its complete cyclic integral of δqrev/T. In the reversible limit, the working material and the reservoir have the same temperature at each heat contact.

Derive the Carnot heat ratio with an ideal gas, without using entropy

Use n moles of ideal gas with approximately constant molar heat capacities. Label the four steps A → B (isothermal expansion at Th), B → C (reversible adiabatic expansion), C → D (isothermal compression at Tc), and D → A (reversible adiabatic compression).

For each isotherm, ideal-gas internal energy is constant. Using the first law and P = nRT/V gives:

\[\begin{aligned}q_h&=nRT_h\ln\frac{V_B}{V_A},\\q_c&=-nRT_c\ln\frac{V_C}{V_D}.\end{aligned}\]

On a reversible adiabat, q = 0, so energy conservation gives nCV,m dT = −P dV. Integrating yields:

\[\frac{dT}{T}=-\frac{R}{C_{V,m}}\frac{dV}{V}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad TV^{\gamma-1}=\text{constant},\qquad\gamma=\frac{C_{P,m}}{C_{V,m}}\]

Apply this relation to the two adiabats and divide their equations:

\[T_hV_B^{\gamma-1}=T_cV_C^{\gamma-1},\qquad T_hV_A^{\gamma-1}=T_cV_D^{\gamma-1}\]
\[\frac{V_B}{V_A}=\frac{V_C}{V_D}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{q_c}{q_h}=-\frac{T_c}{T_h}\]

The volume logarithms cancel. This demonstrates the ratio for an ideal-gas Carnot engine. Carnot’s theorem extends the result to other reversible working substances; the absolute thermodynamic scale agrees with the ideal-gas temperature scale in its ideal limit.

Step 2 · Extend the result to every reversible cycle

A general reversible loop can be approximated by many small Carnot loops. Heat transfers on their shared internal paths cancel in pairs, leaving the outer loop. In the limit of infinitesimal steps, the two-reservoir cancellation becomes Clausius’ equality:

\[\boxed{\oint_{\mathrm{rev}}\frac{\delta q}{T}=0}\]

The circle on the integral means that the process ends where it began. This statement concerns a complete cycle: entropy changes on individual parts can be positive or negative even though their sum around a reversible cycle is zero.

A more general proof using one reference reservoir

Suppose a reversible cyclic device takes signed amounts qi from reservoirs at temperatures Ti. Attach auxiliary reversible Carnot devices that return each qi to its original reservoir while exchanging heat with one reference reservoir at T₀. For each auxiliary device, its heat at Ti is −qi; the Carnot relation gives its reference-reservoir heat q0,i:

\[-\frac{q_i}{T_i}+\frac{q_{0,i}}{T_0}=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad q_0=\sum_iq_{0,i}=T_0\sum_i\frac{q_i}{T_i}\]

All original reservoir exchanges cancel. The combined cyclic apparatus now exchanges heat only with the reference reservoir. A positive q₀ would convert heat from a single reservoir completely into net work, contradicting the Kelvin–Planck statement of the second law. A negative q₀ is also impossible for this reversible apparatus, since reversing it would give a positive q₀. Hence q₀ = 0, so:

\[\sum_i\frac{q_i}{T_i}=0\quad\longrightarrow\quad\oint_{\mathrm{rev}}\frac{\delta q}{T}=0\]

The final limit allows heat contacts at continuously varying temperatures. This proof uses the heat-engine form of the second law, not the entropy formula we are trying to establish.

Step 3 · A zero cyclic integral means a state-function difference

Take two different reversible paths, P and R, between the same equilibrium states A and B. Follow P from A to B and reverse R to return from B to A. The combined path is a reversible cycle, so:

\[0=\int_{A\to B,\,P}\frac{\delta q_{\mathrm{rev}}}{T}+\int_{B\to A,\,R^{-1}}\frac{\delta q_{\mathrm{rev}}}{T}=\int_{A\to B,\,P}\frac{\delta q_{\mathrm{rev}}}{T}-\int_{A\to B,\,R}\frac{\delta q_{\mathrm{rev}}}{T}\]

Thus both forward integrals are equal. The temperature-weighted reversible heat depends only on A and B, even though the heat itself can differ between the paths. Within an equilibrium state space connected by reversible paths, we may therefore define a property S such that:

\[S(B)-S(A)\equiv\int_A^B\frac{\delta q_{\mathrm{rev}}}{T}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\boxed{dS=\frac{\delta q_{\mathrm{rev}}}{T}}\]

Why does the letter S suddenly appear?

At this point we introduce a name and symbol for the state property that the path-independent integral lets us construct. The name is entropy, and its conventional symbol is S. To make the construction explicit, choose one equilibrium reference state O and assign it a value S(O). For every reversibly connected state X, define:

\[S(X)\equiv S(O)+\int_O^X\frac{\delta q_{\mathrm{rev}}}{T}\]

Path independence makes this assignment unambiguous: every reversible route from O to X gives the same value. Subtract the assignments for A and B. A path from O to B may pass through A, so the part from O to A cancels:

\[\begin{aligned}S(B)-S(A)&=\int_O^B\frac{\delta q_{\mathrm{rev}}}{T}-\int_O^A\frac{\delta q_{\mathrm{rev}}}{T}\\&=\int_A^B\frac{\delta q_{\mathrm{rev}}}{T}.\end{aligned}\]

Choosing S(O) = 0 is convenient for calculating differences; it does not mean that O is at absolute zero or that its physical absolute entropy vanishes. A different reference assignment adds the same constant to every state of this fixed material system and leaves all entropy differences unchanged.

The logical sequence is: prove path independence → construct a state function → call it S → write its differential as dS. The symbol ≡ marks the definition. The connection to Boltzmann’s microscopic formula requires a further argument, developed in section 04 below.

In mathematical language, 1/T is an integrating factor: multiplying the inexact heat differential δqrev by 1/T produces the exact differential dS. The symbol δ reminds us that heat is path-dependent; d denotes the change of a state function. This construction sets entropy differences; an additional reference, such as the third-law convention in section 05, sets absolute values.

What has been proved, and what is defined? The second law and the absolute temperature scale establish the path independence of reversible heat divided by T. We give the resulting state function the name entropy. Starting instead with dU = T dS − P dV and using δqrev = dU + P dV recovers the same formula, but that is not an independent derivation because the fundamental relation already contains the entropy construction.

Check the division by temperature

A reversible engine takes 1000 J from a 500 K reservoir and rejects 600 J to a 300 K reservoir. Its hot isotherm changes the working material’s entropy by +1000/500 = +2.00 J K⁻¹. Its cold isotherm changes it by −600/300 = −2.00 J K⁻¹. The adiabats contribute zero, so the cycle returns its entropy to the starting value.

Energy check: the net work output is 400 J and the efficiency is 400/1000 = 0.40, equal to 1 − 300/500. The same heat amount would correspond to a larger entropy transfer at a lower temperature.

Why the equation specifies “reversible”

Entropy is a state function. To calculate its change during an irreversible process, choose a hypothetical reversible path connecting the same equilibrium endpoints. Using the actual heat divided by T is generally wrong.

A useful entropy balance separates transfer from production:

\[\Delta S_{\mathrm{sys}}=\int\frac{\delta q}{T_b}+S_{\mathrm{gen}},\qquad S_{\mathrm{gen}}\geq0\]

Here Tb is the local boundary temperature at which heat crosses, and the integral includes all heat contacts. Entropy can enter or leave with heat, while irreversible processes produce entropy. For an isolated system the transfer term is zero, so its entropy cannot decrease.

\[\Delta S_{\mathrm{universe}}=\Delta S_{\mathrm{sys}}+\Delta S_{\mathrm{surr}}\geq0\]

For an actual spontaneous irreversible process, the total is positive. A reversible limit has zero total production. At stable equilibrium there is no allowed macroscopic change that can further increase the entropy of the isolated whole under its constraints.

Why q = 0 does not imply ΔS = 0

One mole of ideal gas expands freely into a vacuum inside an insulated rigid container, doubling its accessible volume. No heat crosses the outer boundary and no external work is done: q = w = 0. Thus ΔU = 0 and the ideal-gas temperature is unchanged.

For the entropy calculation, use a reversible isothermal expansion between the same endpoints:

\[\Delta S=nR\ln\frac{V_2}{V_1}=R\ln2=5.763\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}\]

Check: the surroundings’ entropy is unchanged; the positive system entropy equals the entropy produced. An adiabatic process is isentropic only if it is also reversible.

04The microscopic meaning: arrangements and energy levels

A macrostate specifies properties such as energy, volume and particle number. A microstate specifies a microscopic configuration consistent with those constraints. For equally probable accessible microstates, Boltzmann’s relation is:

\[S=k_B\ln\Omega,\qquad \Delta S=k_B\ln\frac{\Omega_2}{\Omega_1}\]

Independent systems have multiplied numbers of microstates. Taking the logarithm makes their entropies add. This is why entropy behaves as an extensive property.

When an ideal gas doubles its volume, each particle has roughly twice as many translational position states. For N particles, the ratio is 2N; the indistinguishability factor cancels between the two volumes:

\[\frac{\Omega_2}{\Omega_1}=2^N\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Delta S=Nk_B\ln2=nR\ln2\]

This reproduces the thermodynamic expansion result. It also clarifies the limitations of “disorder”: visual untidiness is not a quantitative state count. Entropy includes the population of translational, rotational, vibrational and electronic states, not just positions in a drawing.

Gases usually have greater molar entropy than their liquid or solid forms because their accessible states differ greatly. More gas molecules often suggests a positive reaction entropy. Neither rule replaces tabulated data when different molecules, temperatures, pressures or solvation effects are involved.

How do we know this is the same entropy?

We now follow route B from the two-route comparison: start with microscopic entropy, obtain statistical temperature, then connect these to route A. Give the two entropy constructions temporary labels so that their agreement is something to establish:

ConstructionStarting pointMeaning
Clausius entropy, SCl\(dS_{\mathrm{Cl}}=\delta q_{\mathrm{rev}}/T\)A macroscopic state function constructed from reversible heat measurements.
Boltzmann entropy, SB\(S_B=k_B\ln\Omega\)A microscopic expression obtained by counting accessible states in a specified macrostate.

The common symbol is justified by checking their measurable changes. If a microscopic entropy has the same differential as SCl along every reversible path in a connected equilibrium region, then their difference is a constant for the fixed material system:

\[dS_{\mathrm{stat}}=\frac{\delta q_{\mathrm{rev}}}{T}=dS_{\mathrm{Cl}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad S_{\mathrm{stat}}-S_{\mathrm{Cl}}=C\]

Matching the reference then fixes C. For ordinary macroscopic equilibrium matter, statistical mechanics supplies this connection. We can see both the general equilibrium calculation and a concrete gas example.

Bridge 1 · How counting states identifies temperature

Let two weakly interacting systems exchange energy while their combined energy stays fixed. In the equilibrium counting model, the most probable energy split maximizes the product of their state counts, or equivalently the sum of their logarithms:

\[\begin{aligned}\Omega_{\mathrm{tot}}(U_A)&=\Omega_A(U_A)\Omega_B(U_{\mathrm{tot}}-U_A),\\0&=\frac{d\ln\Omega_{\mathrm{tot}}}{dU_A}\\&=\frac{\partial\ln\Omega_A}{\partial U_A}-\frac{\partial\ln\Omega_B}{\partial U_B}.\end{aligned}\]

Thus the energy derivative of kB ln Ω is equal in bodies at thermal equilibrium. Its reciprocal defines a statistical temperature:

\[\frac1{T_{\mathrm{stat}}}\equiv\left(\frac{\partial S_B}{\partial U}\right)_{V,N}\]

This explains its thermal-equilibrium role. Matching it to the absolute Kelvin scale also requires calibration: for a classical monatomic ideal gas, state counting gives Tstat = 2U/(3NkB), while mechanics gives P = 2U/(3V). Together they give PV = NkBTstat, the ideal-gas thermometer relation. With the matching temperature scale and the factor kB = R/NA, the statistical T agrees with the thermodynamic T used here. Merely defining a derivative would leave this physical identification unexplained.

Bridge 2 · From Boltzmann counting to general probabilities

For equilibrium in contact with a large heat bath, system microstates generally have unequal probabilities. The corresponding Gibbs statistical entropy is:

\[S_G=-k_B\sum_i p_i\ln p_i\]

It includes the equal-probability Boltzmann formula as an exact special case. If precisely Ω accessible states each have probability 1/Ω, substitution gives:

\[S_G=-k_B\sum_{i=1}^{\Omega}\frac1\Omega\ln\frac1\Omega=k_B\ln\Omega=S_B\]

For a system weakly coupled to a large reservoir, the reservoir’s available states weight a system microstate of energy Ei by exp[−Ei/(kBT)]. Normalization gives the canonical equilibrium probabilities:

\[p_i=\frac{e^{-E_i/(k_BT)}}{Z},\qquad Z=\sum_i e^{-E_i/(k_BT)},\qquad U=\sum_i p_iE_i\]

The temperature is the calibrated reservoir temperature from bridge 1. A slowly controlled reversible process traces successive equilibrium distributions; it may also change energy levels by changing an external parameter such as the volume.

Bridge 3 · Show that statistical entropy changes by reversible heat divided by T

Keep the particle species and their amounts fixed. Differentiate the average energy. Changing the level energies describes mechanical work on the system; changing their populations describes the heat transfer in this quasistatic equilibrium model:

\[\begin{aligned}dU&=\sum_i E_i\,dp_i+\sum_i p_i\,dE_i,\\\delta q_{\mathrm{rev}}&=\sum_i E_i\,dp_i,\qquad\delta w_{\mathrm{on,rev}}=\sum_i p_i\,dE_i.\end{aligned}\]

This is the first-law energy balance at the microscopic level. For a box changing volume, the work term is −P dV. Now differentiate SG. Since probabilities always sum to one, their changes sum to zero:

\[\begin{aligned}dS_G&=-k_B\sum_i(\ln p_i+1)\,dp_i\\&=-k_B\sum_i\ln p_i\,dp_i,\qquad\sum_i dp_i=0.\end{aligned}\]

Insert ln pi = −Ei/(kBT) − ln Z at the current equilibrium state. The normalization term again vanishes:

\[\begin{aligned}dS_G&=\frac1T\sum_i E_i\,dp_i+k_B\ln Z\sum_i dp_i\\&=\frac1T\sum_i E_i\,dp_i\\&=\boxed{\frac{\delta q_{\mathrm{rev}}}{T}}=dS_{\mathrm{Cl}}.\end{aligned}\]

The equality establishes agreement with the macroscopic heat definition for reversible equilibrium changes within this weak-coupling model. At equal probabilities, SG reduces to Boltzmann’s formula. For macroscopic equilibrium systems where ensembles are equivalent, the microcanonical count and canonical entropy give the same thermodynamic entropy to leading extensive order.

Concrete independent check: count both position and momentum states of an ideal gas

For a classical monatomic ideal gas, the positions contribute a factor VN to the state count. The allowed momenta lie in a 3N-dimensional energy region. Its state count has leading energy dependence U3N/2. In the macroscopic limit, write the resulting entropy at fixed N as:

\[S_B\simeq Nk_B\ln\frac{V}{V_{\mathrm{ref}}}+\frac{3N}{2}k_B\ln\frac{U}{U_{\mathrm{ref}}}+C_N\]

The reference volume and energy keep logarithms dimensionless. Terms independent of U and V, including the identical-particle counting factor, are collected in CN. Energy-shell details produce subextensive corrections, omitted in this macroscopic expression.

Differentiate the counting result, then use the mechanical ideal-gas relations U = 3NkBT/2 and PV = NkBT:

\[\begin{aligned}dS_B&=\frac{Nk_B}{V}\,dV+\frac{3Nk_B}{2U}\,dU\\&=\frac{P}{T}\,dV+\frac1T\,dU\\&=\frac{dU+P\,dV}{T}.\end{aligned}\]

The first law on a reversible PV-work path gives dU = δqrev − P dV. Therefore the same microscopic count yields:

\[dS_B=\frac{\delta q_{\mathrm{rev}}}{T}=dS_{\mathrm{Cl}}\quad\text{in the macroscopic ideal-gas limit}\]

This checks both heating and expansion, beyond the earlier isothermal volume-doubling example. At fixed volume, ΔS = (3/2)nR ln(T₂/T₁), agreeing with the heat-capacity calculation using CV,m = 3R/2.

What does “exactly the same” mean?

  • Same entropy changes: within the stated reversible equilibrium model, the statistical calculation obeys the Clausius heat relation. The difference of the two state functions is therefore constant for a fixed material system.
  • Same absolute reference: for a unique quantum ground state, Ω = 1 and kB ln Ω = 0. Matching this to the perfect-crystal third-law reference fixes the constant. A ground-state degeneracy g₀ instead contributes residual entropy kB ln g₀.
  • Consistent state counts: identical particles must be counted correctly, and classical phase-space volumes need a dimensionless measure. Quantum mechanics supplies the state-counting normalization. Reactions also require consistent references across species.
  • Scope of the macroscopic statement: a finite microcanonical system and a finite canonical system have different energy constraints and generally different entropies. For ordinary short-range systems in the thermodynamic limit, their entropy per particle agrees at corresponding equilibrium states. Small systems, long-range interactions and nonequilibrium distributions require additional care.

The shared symbol S is earned by matching the heat relation, temperature scale and reference. Boltzmann’s counting formula supplies a microscopic explanation of the macroscopic state function under its equilibrium assumptions.

Why does higher entropy correspond to the more probable macrostate?

In the equal-probability model, the probability of a macrostate is proportional to the number of microstates that realize it. If macrostate B has more entropy than A, their probability ratio is:

\[\frac{P_B}{P_A}=\exp\left(\frac{S_B-S_A}{k_B}\right)\]

For macroscopic numbers of particles this ratio can be enormous. Microscopic dynamics can remain reversible while macroscopic departures from the overwhelmingly probable equilibrium state become extraordinarily unlikely.

05The third law and practical entropy calculations

The zeroth law makes temperature a consistent equilibrium property: systems in thermal equilibrium with the same reference are in thermal equilibrium with one another. The third law supplies an entropy reference: the entropy of a perfect crystal with a unique ground state approaches zero as T approaches 0 K.

Heat a substance reversibly from that reference, adding the entropy of every phase transition. The standard molar entropy at a specified temperature is built from:

\[S_m^\circ(T)=\sum_{\mathrm{phases}}\int\frac{C_{P,m}^\circ(T')}{T'}\,dT'+\sum_{\mathrm{transitions}}\frac{\Delta_{\mathrm{tr}}H_m^\circ}{T_{\mathrm{tr}}}\]

The integrals cover the appropriate temperature intervals in each phase, from the low-temperature limit to the final T. Transition terms apply to reversible phase changes at their coexistence temperatures. Frozen configurational disorder or ground-state degeneracy can require a residual-entropy contribution; “every solid has zero entropy” is incorrect.

Three equations used repeatedly

Process or dataEntropy calculationConditions
Heating within one phase\(\Delta S=nC_{P,m}\ln(T_2/T_1)\)Constant pressure, fixed composition, approximately constant molar heat capacity.
Reversible phase transition\(\Delta S=\Delta H_{\mathrm{tr}}/T_{\mathrm{tr}}\)At the equilibrium transition temperature and pressure.
Reaction from a table\(\Delta_rS^\circ=\sum_i\nu_iS_{m,i}^\circ\)All values at the same T, using the stated phases and balanced coefficients.

The element trap. At 298 K, N₂(g), H₂(g) and other reference-state elements have nonzero standard molar entropies. It is their standard formation enthalpy and formation Gibbs energy that are assigned zero. Never substitute zero for their S° values.

06Derive Gibbs energy from the two laws

Take a closed reacting system in contact with a large heat reservoir at fixed temperature T and a constant-pressure environment. The initial and final system states are at that T and P. Assume only PV work, with no electrical or shaft work extracted.

  1. Use the first law to identify the heat. At constant pressure, the system absorbs qsys = ΔHsys. The reservoir receives the opposite heat, qsurr = −ΔHsys.
  2. Calculate the reservoir’s entropy change. Its temperature stays at T, so the entropy lost or gained by it is heat received divided by T.
\[\Delta S_{\mathrm{surr}}=-\frac{\Delta H_{\mathrm{sys}}}{T}\]
  1. Apply the second law to system plus surroundings. Add the system’s state-function entropy change to the reservoir’s change.
\[\Delta S_{\mathrm{universe}}=\Delta S_{\mathrm{sys}}-\frac{\Delta H_{\mathrm{sys}}}{T}\]
  1. Multiply by −T and define a system state function. Because T is positive, this multiplication reverses the spontaneity inequality.
\[\begin{aligned}G&\equiv H-TS,\\\Delta G&=\Delta H-T\Delta S\\&=-T\Delta S_{\mathrm{universe}}\leq0.\end{aligned}\]

This is the physical origin of the competition. The ΔH contribution accounts for the surroundings’ entropy effect; the −TΔS contribution accounts for the system’s entropy effect in energy units. They are two parts of one total-entropy criterion.

At fixed T and PInterpretation for an allowed infinitesimal change
dG < 0The change can proceed spontaneously under these constraints.
dG > 0The reverse change lowers G; forward change requires a drive or coupling.
dG = 0 at a minimumStable equilibrium with respect to the allowed variation.

Two necessary qualifications. The identity G = H − TS always defines G, but the finite form ΔG = ΔH − TΔS needs equal endpoint temperatures. If temperature changes, use ΔG = ΔH − Δ(TS). The equality with −TΔSuniverse also needs the reservoir and work conditions above. A driven electrolysis cell or a freely cooling isolated system needs its appropriate full balance.

A zero finite ΔG between two arbitrarily selected states does not itself prove that either is a stable equilibrium. Equilibrium is a constrained minimum, not merely equal endpoint values.

07The university framework: fundamental relations

For a simple compressible equilibrium system, allowing composition changes, the fundamental relation is:

\[dU=T\,dS-P\,dV+\sum_i\mu_i\,dn_i\]

The chemical potential μi describes how adding species i changes the energy under the relevant constraints. This relation connects neighbouring equilibrium states; it does not say that actual irreversible heat is always T dS.

Different laboratory constraints call for different thermodynamic potentials. A Legendre transform replaces one natural variable by its conjugate: adding PV replaces volume with pressure, while subtracting TS replaces entropy with temperature.

\[\begin{aligned}H&=U+PV,&dH&=T\,dS+V\,dP+\sum_i\mu_i\,dn_i,\\A&=U-TS,&dA&=-S\,dT-P\,dV+\sum_i\mu_i\,dn_i,\\G&=U+PV-TS,&dG&=-S\,dT+V\,dP+\sum_i\mu_i\,dn_i.\end{aligned}\]
Constraints for a closed systemStable equilibrium criterion
Fixed U and V; fixed conserved material totalsMaximize S.
Fixed S and V; fixed conserved material totalsMinimize U.
Fixed T and V; no non-PV workMinimize Helmholtz energy A (also written F).
Fixed T and P; no non-PV workMinimize Gibbs energy G.

These are constrained criteria. A closed reacting system keeps its elemental totals fixed while individual species amounts can change. At fixed T and P, the dG expression reduces to a composition term, which leads directly to chemical equilibrium in section 11.

Why temperature and pressure change the balance

At fixed composition, read derivatives directly from dG:

\[\left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial T}\right)_{P,\{n_i\}}=-S,\qquad\left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial P}\right)_{T,\{n_i\}}=V\]

A high-entropy phase has a more negative Gibbs-energy slope versus temperature. A high-volume phase has a larger positive slope versus pressure. This explains why heating often favours a gas while compression often favours a condensed phase.

08The four sign combinations and temperature

For the reaction as written, compare ΔᵣH° and TΔᵣS° in the same energy units. The table describes standard reaction Gibbs energy, equivalent to the actual reaction driving force when Q = 1. Actual composition enters later.

ΔᵣH°ΔᵣS°Effect on ΔᵣG° = ΔᵣH° − TΔᵣS°Result in this model
−+Both terms are negative.ΔᵣG° < 0 at every positive T.
+−Both terms are positive.ΔᵣG° > 0 at every positive T.

++Positive enthalpy competes with a negative entropy contribution.ΔᵣG° < 0 at high T.

For same-sign, nonzero values, the standard-state crossover occurs where the two terms have equal magnitude:

\[\Delta_rG^\circ=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad T_*\approx\frac{\Delta_rH^\circ}{\Delta_rS^\circ}\]

The ratio must have consistent units and yield kelvin. A positive crossover exists only if numerator and denominator have the same sign. “At every T” in the table assumes the signs persist and the phases remain valid; constant ΔᵣH° and ΔᵣS° are only an approximation over a limited range.

If ΔᵣS° = 0, this constant-property model gives ΔᵣG° = ΔᵣH° with no temperature competition. If ΔᵣH° = 0, the sign is set by −TΔᵣS° at positive T. These limiting cases are useful algebra checks.

Crossover is not a yield prediction. For a reaction, ΔᵣG° = 0 means K = 1. It does not mean equal concentrations, 50% conversion, or equilibrium at every composition. For two pure phases at the specified pressure, a transition condition is instead equality of their chemical potentials.

09Three worked examples

A. Endothermic and favourable

Use an illustrative reaction with ΔᵣH° = +20.0 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔᵣS° = +100 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. At 298 K and Q = 1:

\[\begin{aligned}\Delta_rS^\circ&=0.100\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}},\\\Delta_rG^\circ&=20.0-298(0.100)\\&=-9.80\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}.\end{aligned}\]

For a one-mole standard-state conversion under the reservoir model, the system gains 100 J K⁻¹ while the surroundings lose 20,000/298 = 67.1 J K⁻¹. The total is +32.9 J K⁻¹.

Independent check: −298(32.9) J ≈ −9.80 kJ. The system’s entropy gain exceeds the reservoir’s loss. The model crossover is 200 K; at 150 K the same approximation gives ΔᵣG° = +5.00 kJ mol⁻¹.

B. Exothermic and unfavourable

For a second illustrative reaction, let ΔᵣH° = −10.0 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔᵣS° = −50.0 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. At 298 K and Q = 1:

\[\Delta_rG^\circ=-10.0-298(-0.0500)=+4.90\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\]

The system loses 50.0 J K⁻¹ per mole of reaction, while released heat gives the reservoir only 33.6 J K⁻¹. The proposed forward conversion would reduce total entropy by 16.4 J K⁻¹ per mole, so it cannot proceed unaided at Q = 1.

Check: at 150 K, ΔᵣG° = −2.50 kJ mol⁻¹. Cooling reduces the energetic weight of the negative system entropy change. Heat release alone never settles the question.

C. Melting ice near 1 bar

For one mole of ice melting at its equilibrium melting temperature, approximately 273.15 K, use ΔHfus ≈ 6.01 kJ mol⁻¹. The liquid has higher entropy:

\[\Delta S_{\mathrm{fus}}=\frac{6010}{273.15}\approx22.0\ \mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\]

At coexistence, ΔGfus = 0. A little above this temperature the liquid has lower molar G; a little below it the solid does. Holding the rounded values constant at 280 K gives:

\[\Delta G_{\mathrm{fus}}\approx6.01-280(0.0220)=-0.150\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\]

Check: the positive melting entropy makes the liquid’s G fall faster with T. Freezing reverses both ΔH and ΔS and can be spontaneous below the melting point despite decreasing the system’s entropy. Supercooling is a kinetic qualification.

What does increasing pressure change?

For ideal gases, activities are partial pressures divided by the standard pressure:

\[Q=\frac{(p_{\ce{NH3}}/p^\circ)^2}{(p_{\ce{N2}}/p^\circ)(p_{\ce{H2}}/p^\circ)^3}\]

At fixed T and initial mole fractions, multiplying every partial pressure by a factor f changes Q by f−2. Compression therefore lowers Q, favouring forward reaction relative to equilibrium. The thermodynamic K at this T is unchanged in the ideal-gas model. At high pressure, fugacities replace ideal partial pressures.

11Why composition matters: chemical potential, Q and K

Reaction Gibbs energy is a local slope, not the total Gibbs energy of a bottle. Let the reaction extent ξ increase in the forward direction, with dni = νi dξ. At fixed T and P:

\[dG=\sum_i\mu_i\nu_i\,d\xi=\Delta_rG\,d\xi,\qquad\Delta_rG=\left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial\xi}\right)_{T,P}=\sum_i\nu_i\mu_i\]

Each species’ chemical potential depends on its activity, a dimensionless effective concentration. Write:

\[\mu_i=\mu_i^\circ+RT\ln a_i,\qquad Q=\prod_i a_i^{\nu_i}\]

Substitute into the stoichiometric sum. The standard terms combine into ΔᵣG°; the logarithms combine into a product with stoichiometric exponents:

\[\boxed{\Delta_rG=\Delta_rG^\circ+RT\ln Q}\]

At an interior equilibrium where forward and reverse changes are both available, the slope vanishes. Setting Q = K gives:

\[\Delta_rG^\circ=-RT\ln K,\qquad\boxed{\Delta_rG=RT\ln\frac{Q}{K}}\]
Current compositionSign of ΔᵣGDirection lowering G
Q < KNegativeForward.
Q = KZeroNo net reaction at equilibrium.
Q > KPositiveReverse.

A positive ΔᵣG° means K < 1; it does not prohibit forward reaction from a sufficiently reactant-rich composition. A negative ΔᵣG° means K > 1; it does not mean all reactants vanish. In either case a changing composition changes Q.

Same standard energy, different direction

Take an illustrative ΔᵣG° = +5.00 kJ mol⁻¹ at 298 K. Then K = exp[−5000/(R × 298)] ≈ 0.133. Start instead with Q = 0.001:

\[\Delta_rG=5.00+\frac{8.314(298)\ln(0.001)}{1000}\approx-12.11\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\]

Check: Q < K, so the forward direction is favourable despite positive ΔᵣG°. The available mixture must be specified before predicting direction.

See the Gibbs minimum explicitly for A ⇌ B

Consider an ideal one-to-one mixture with fixed total amount n, and let x be the mole fraction of B. Apart from a composition-independent constant:

\[\frac{G(x)}{n}=x\Delta_rG^\circ+RT\big[x\ln x+(1-x)\ln(1-x)\big]\]
\[\frac{d(G/n)}{dx}=\Delta_rG^\circ+RT\ln\frac{x}{1-x},\qquad\frac{d^2(G/n)}{dx^2}=RT\left(\frac1x+\frac1{1-x}\right)>0\]

The positive curvature establishes a minimum for 0 < x < 1. At the minimum, x/(1 − x) = K, giving x = K/(1 + K). Only this special one-to-one model has x = 1/2 when K = 1; other stoichiometries have different relations.

More generally, a finite composition change obeys ΔG = ∫ ΔᵣG dξ. Multiplying an initial reaction slope by the total extent is wrong if the slope changes appreciably during reaction.

13Beyond constant enthalpy and entropy

Temperature affects both reaction properties through heat capacities. Define the stoichiometric heat-capacity difference:

\[\Delta_rC_P^\circ=\sum_i\nu_i C_{P,m,i}^\circ\]
\[\begin{aligned}\Delta_rH^\circ(T)&=\Delta_rH^\circ(T_0)+\int_{T_0}^{T}\Delta_rC_P^\circ(T')\,dT',\\\Delta_rS^\circ(T)&=\Delta_rS^\circ(T_0)+\int_{T_0}^{T}\frac{\Delta_rC_P^\circ(T')}{T'}\,dT'.\end{aligned}\]

The first is Kirchhoff’s relation. With constant ΔᵣC°P, the corrections become ΔᵣC°P(T − T₀) and ΔᵣC°P ln(T/T₀). If a phase transition occurs, split the temperature path and include transition enthalpy and entropy.

Worked correction: how much does heat capacity change the answer?

Use the illustrative endothermic reaction from example A at T₀ = 298 K, adding ΔᵣC°P = −50.0 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. Estimate its properties at 500 K, assuming no phase change:

\[\begin{aligned}\Delta_rH^\circ(500)&=20.0-0.0500(500-298)=9.90\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}},\\\Delta_rS^\circ(500)&=100-50.0\ln(500/298)\\&\approx74.1\ \mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}},\\\Delta_rG^\circ(500)&\approx9.90-500(0.0741)\\&\approx-27.2\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}.\end{aligned}\]

Holding the original H and S constant would give −30.0 kJ mol⁻¹. The correction changes the magnitude, although not the sign in this example.

Gibbs–Helmholtz and van ’t Hoff

At fixed standard pressure, differentiating reaction Gibbs energy gives dΔᵣG°/dT = −ΔᵣS°. Combine that with ΔᵣG° = ΔᵣH° − TΔᵣS°:

\[\frac{d(\Delta_rG^\circ/T)}{dT}=-\frac{\Delta_rH^\circ}{T^2}\]

Now substitute ΔᵣG°/T = −R ln K. The result predicts how equilibrium responds to temperature:

\[\frac{d\ln K}{dT}=\frac{\Delta_rH^\circ}{RT^2}\]
\[\ln\frac{K_2}{K_1}\approx-\frac{\Delta_rH^\circ}{R}\left(\frac1{T_2}-\frac1{T_1}\right)\]

The differential form uses the enthalpy at each temperature. The integrated form assumes it is approximately constant. Heating increases K for an endothermic reaction and decreases K for an exothermic one. This gives a quantitative foundation for the usual heat-side form of Le Châtelier’s principle.

14Mixing and dissolution: why “more particles” is incomplete

For an ideal mixture formed from distinct pure components at the same T and P, unlike and like interactions are thermodynamically equivalent in the ideal model. The enthalpy of mixing is zero, but each component gains accessible configurations.

\[\Delta H_{\mathrm{mix}}=0,\qquad\Delta S_{\mathrm{mix}}=-nR\sum_i x_i\ln x_i\]
\[\Delta G_{\mathrm{mix}}=nRT\sum_i x_i\ln x_i<0\]

Because 0 < xi < 1, each logarithm is negative and the mixing entropy is positive. For one mole each of two different ideal gases, n = 2 mol and x₁ = x₂ = 1/2. Thus ΔSmix = 2R ln 2 = 11.53 J K⁻¹ and ΔGmix ≈ −3.44 kJ at 298 K.

Combining two portions of the same gas at identical T and P does not create a distinguishable mixture and gives no mixing entropy. This distinction prevents double-counting identical particles.

A salt dissolving is more than ideal mixing

Dissolution involves separating ions from a lattice, reorganizing solvent and forming ion–solvent interactions. Hydration can constrain water molecules and reduce their entropy. The net entropy of dissolution may be positive or negative; counting dissolved ions alone is insufficient.

Likewise, the enthalpy balances energy needed to disrupt the solid and solvent structure against energy released by new interactions. Dissolution may be endothermic or exothermic. Its direction still depends on the total Gibbs-energy balance at the current activities.

\[\Delta_rG=RT\ln\frac{Q}{K_{sp}}\]

Mixing contributions make the chemical potentials concentration-dependent, explaining why dissolution can become less favourable as a solution fills with ions. Read the Ksp lesson’s ideal-mixing derivation for the link from entropy to μ = μ° + RT ln a.

15The deeper origin: energy, probability and free energy

The equal-probability formula S = kB ln Ω applies to a fixed-energy ensemble. A system in thermal contact with a reservoir instead samples microstates with different energies. Its equilibrium microstate probabilities follow the Boltzmann distribution:

\[p_j=\frac{e^{-E_j/(k_BT)}}{Z},\qquad Z=\sum_j e^{-E_j/(k_BT)}\]

Z is the canonical partition function: a sum over the system’s microstates. Lower-energy states receive larger weight individually; a higher-energy macrostate can still be more probable if vastly more microstates realize it. Degeneracy, the number of states at a given energy, is therefore crucial.

For general probabilities, Gibbs’ entropy formula is:

\[S=-k_B\sum_jp_j\ln p_j\]

Substituting ln pj = −Ej/(kBT) − ln Z, using Σpj = 1 and U = ΣpjEj, gives:

\[S=\frac{U}{T}+k_B\ln Z\quad\Longrightarrow\quad A=U-TS=-k_BT\ln Z\]

This derivation explains why free energy combines an energy term and an entropy term. At fixed T and V, equilibrium probabilities minimize Helmholtz energy. At fixed T and P, the analogous isothermal–isobaric ensemble leads to Gibbs energy; pressure–volume effects change the relevant energy accounting from U to H.

A two-macrostate probability argument for Gibbs energy

Consider two equilibrium macrostates of the same closed material at fixed T and P. In a coarse-grained model, let each have enthalpy Hi and intrinsic entropy Si. The state count contributes exp(Si/kB), while the reservoir weight contributes exp[−Hi/(kBT)]. Thus:

\[P_i\propto e^{S_i/k_B}e^{-H_i/(k_BT)}=e^{-G_i/(k_BT)}\]
\[\frac{P_B}{P_A}=\exp\left[-\frac{G_B-G_A}{k_BT}\right]\]

These are energies and entropies for the whole model system, so kB appears. Replacing it by R is appropriate only when consistently using molar quantities in a corresponding molecular or reaction model. Counting degeneracy and then also inserting an entropy that already counts it would double-count states.

Temperature sets the exchange rate between entropy and energy in the free-energy balance. This is why the relative importance of the entropy term grows with T, and why an energy-only argument misses much of chemical equilibrium.

16Direction, speed, useful work and coupling

Why a favourable reaction can be slow

ΔᵣG compares the free energies on either side of an infinitesimal reaction advance. A rate depends on reaching a transition state, described by an activation free energy ΔG‡. In a simple transition-state model:

\[k\approx\kappa\frac{k_BT}{h}\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta G^\ddagger}{RT}\right)\]

Here h is Planck’s constant, κ is a transmission factor, and ΔG‡ is molar. The exact rate model depends on the mechanism. A catalyst provides a faster pathway but does not change the reactant and product state functions, ΔᵣG°, or K. At equilibrium, microscopic forward and reverse reactions continue at equal rates.

What “free” energy means operationally

At fixed T and P, a reversible process can deliver a maximum amount of non-PV work equal to the decrease in Gibbs energy. With work output counted positive:

\[w_{\mathrm{nonPV,out}}\leq-\Delta G\]

An ideal reversible galvanic cell converts this decrease into electrical work; dissipation reduces useful output. If a nonspontaneous change is electrically driven, the external work and its source must be included in the total balance. The no-non-PV-work assumption used to equate −ΔG/T with the reservoir-model entropy production no longer applies to the system alone.

How an uphill process can be driven by another

Because G is a state function, Gibbs-energy changes add for reactions combined at matching conditions. A +10 kJ mol⁻¹ step can be driven by a −30 kJ mol⁻¹ step if a mechanism enforces the coupled stoichiometry, giving −20 kJ mol⁻¹ overall. Merely putting the two reactions next to each other does not guarantee coupling.

Biological synthesis and active transport use this principle. The actual driving force depends on concentrations and chemical potentials, so an in-cell hydrolysis energy is not automatically equal to its tabulated standard value.

Open systems can stay ordered without violating the second law

An organism or a continuously operated reactor exchanges matter as well as heat and work. Matter carries entropy. Its balance has the schematic form:

\[\frac{dS_{\mathrm{sys}}}{dt}=\sum_k\frac{\dot q_k}{T_k}+\sum_{\mathrm{in}}\dot n_i\bar S_i-\sum_{\mathrm{out}}\dot n_i\bar S_i+\dot S_{\mathrm{gen}}\]

A steady system can maintain constant entropy while producing entropy internally and exporting it. The closed-system Gibbs criterion should not be applied to such a system without accounting for its flows.

17Use the ideas to solve problems

  1. Write the process with phases and coefficients. State the system, constraints, temperature and whether the question concerns standard states or an actual mixture.
  2. Find the relevant state changes. Calculate reaction enthalpy and entropy from consistent data. Use coefficients and keep elemental molar entropies.
  3. Use the right criterion. For fixed T and P use Gibbs energy; for fixed T and V use Helmholtz energy; for an isolated whole use its entropy.
  4. Check units before subtracting. Convert entropy from J to kJ if enthalpy is in kJ. Always use kelvin in TΔS, logarithms and equilibrium equations.
  5. Include composition when needed. Calculate K from ΔᵣG° and use Q/K to determine the current direction. Solve mass balance and equilibrium together to find yield.
  6. Assess the approximation. Check phases, heat-capacity changes, activities and kinetic barriers. Report a crossover or a rate prediction only within the model that supports it.
Check 1 · Can the system’s entropy decrease spontaneously?

Yes. Freezing below the melting temperature can lower the system’s entropy while heat release raises the surroundings’ entropy by more. The second law requires nonnegative total entropy production, not positive entropy change for every subsystem.

Check 2 · ΔᵣH° = −40 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔᵣS° = −100 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. What happens at 300 K and 500 K?

Convert −100 J to −0.100 kJ mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. At 300 K, ΔᵣG° = −40 + 30 = −10 kJ mol⁻¹. At 500 K it is −40 + 50 = +10 kJ mol⁻¹. The approximate crossover is 400 K. These signs determine direction at Q = 1, not at every mixture composition.

Check 3 · At equilibrium, which of ΔᵣG, ΔᵣG°, ΔH and ΔS must be zero?

For an interior chemical equilibrium, ΔᵣG = 0. ΔᵣG° = −RT ln K is zero only if K = 1. Reaction enthalpy and entropy need not be zero. At equilibrium they can balance through the composition-dependent Gibbs energy; equilibrium does not require every thermodynamic change to vanish separately.

Check 4 · Does ΔᵣH° < 0 and ΔᵣS° > 0 imply complete reaction?

No. The signs imply ΔᵣG° < 0 for the stated temperature and phases, hence K > 1. The magnitude sets how large K is. Composition changes raise Q until equilibrium or a material constraint is reached; neither an exactly complete reaction nor a fast rate follows from signs alone.

Check 5 · Why cannot ΔH and ΔS be compared directly?

Their units differ. Multiply ΔS by absolute temperature to obtain energy units, then compare ΔH with TΔS. Multiplying by T also reflects the reservoir entropy cost of exchanging energy at that temperature.

Enthalpy and entropy enter the same balance because the system exchanges energy with its surroundings. Gibbs energy packages that balance for fixed-temperature, fixed-pressure chemistry; chemical potentials extend it to the mixture actually present.
