SOLUBILITY EQUILIBRIUM · A THERMODYNAMIC DERIVATION

Why does Ksp
have this form?

The second law gives the equilibrium condition. Mixing explains the concentration dependence. Together, they lead to the familiar solubility product.

The question that starts this lesson

“In tutorial.html, how is Ksp = [M]a[X]b derived from thermodynamic law?”

\[\ce{M_{a}X_{b}(s) <=> aM(aq) + bX(aq)}\]
\[K_{sp}=\alpha_{M,\mathrm{eq}}^{a}\alpha_{X,\mathrm{eq}}^{b}\approx\left(\frac{[M]_{\mathrm{eq}}}{c^\circ}\right)^a\left(\frac{[X]_{\mathrm{eq}}}{c^\circ}\right)^b\]

Coefficients a and b become exponents. Ion charges are omitted in this generic equation; an actual dissolution equation must balance charge.

Read 01–06 for the thermodynamic derivation. Sections 07–09 explain mixing. For an alternative starting directly from microscopic arrangements and their probabilities, jump to section 10 — the state-counting derivation. All equations are available offline.

01Start with the equilibrium condition

The first law accounts for energy transfers. The second law determines the direction in which a process can proceed spontaneously: the total entropy of the system and its surroundings increases, reaching a maximum at equilibrium under the appropriate constraints.

For a closed system at constant temperature and pressure, exchanging heat with a reservoir at the same temperature and doing only pressure–volume work, combine the two laws using Gibbs free energy:

\[G=H-TS,\qquad \Delta S_{\mathrm{universe}}=\Delta S_{\mathrm{system}}-\frac{\Delta H_{\mathrm{system}}}{T}=-\frac{\Delta G_{\mathrm{system}}}{T}\]

Spontaneous change therefore lowers the system’s Gibbs free energy. At fixed temperature and pressure, equilibrium is the minimum of G with respect to the allowed composition changes.

At dissolution equilibrium, with solid and solution both present

\[\boxed{\Delta_rG=\left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial\xi}\right)_{T,P}=0}\]

The reaction extent \(\xi\) measures how much of the written reaction has occurred. \(\Delta_rG\) is the slope of G against reaction extent: the Gibbs-energy change per mole of reaction at the current composition.

Keep these two quantities distinct. At equilibrium, \(\Delta_rG=0\). The standard reaction Gibbs energy \(\Delta_rG^\circ\) usually is not zero; it helps determine where the equilibrium lies.

02Give each species a chemical potential

A species’ chemical potential is its partial molar Gibbs energy: how much G changes when a small amount of that species is added while temperature, pressure and the amounts of the other species are fixed.

\[\mu_i=\left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial n_i}\right)_{T,P,n_{j\ne i}}=\mu_i^\circ+RT\ln\alpha_i\]

Activity \(\alpha_i\) is a dimensionless effective concentration. It captures how a species’ chemical potential changes with composition. The logarithmic form is connected to mixing; the ideal-mixture derivation appears in section 08.

Notation used in the derivation
SymbolMeaning
\(a,b\)Coefficients in the balanced dissolution equation.
\(\alpha_i\)Activity; a different symbol avoids confusion with coefficient a.
\(\mu_i^\circ\)Chemical potential in the chosen standard state.
\(R,T\)Gas constant (8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹) and absolute temperature in kelvin.
\(c^\circ\)Standard concentration, 1 mol L⁻¹, for the concentration-based activity convention.

A lower activity gives a more negative logarithmic contribution. Adding an ion to a dilute solution therefore has a different Gibbs-energy cost from adding it to a concentrated solution.

03Let stoichiometry produce the powers

For one mole of the generic salt dissolving, a moles of M and b moles of X appear, while one mole of solid disappears. Add the product contributions and subtract the reactant contribution:

\[\Delta_rG=a\mu_M+b\mu_X-\mu_{\mathrm{solid}}\]

Substitute the activity expression for each chemical potential. The standard-state terms form \(\Delta_rG^\circ=a\mu_M^\circ+b\mu_X^\circ-\mu_{\mathrm{solid}}^\circ\):

\[\begin{aligned}\Delta_rG&=\Delta_rG^\circ+RT\left(a\ln\alpha_M+b\ln\alpha_X-\ln\alpha_{\mathrm{solid}}\right)\\&=\Delta_rG^\circ+RT\ln\left(\frac{\alpha_M^a\alpha_X^b}{\alpha_{\mathrm{solid}}}\right).\end{aligned}\]

The activity of the pure solid is one in the usual pure-phase convention, so its logarithmic contribution vanishes. Define the dissolution reaction quotient Q using the current ion activities:

\[Q=\alpha_M^a\alpha_X^b,\qquad \boxed{\Delta_rG=\Delta_rG^\circ+RT\ln Q}\]

The exponents follow directly from the logarithm identity:

\[a\ln x+b\ln y=\ln(x^ay^b)\]

Why the solid disappears. Its activity is one, not zero. Adding more of the same pure solid does not change its chemical potential at fixed temperature and pressure. The equilibrium condition requires some solid to remain; an unsaturated solution need not satisfy Q = Ksp.

04Set the reaction driving force to zero

At equilibrium, use \(\Delta_rG=0\) and evaluate Q using the equilibrium composition:

\[0=\Delta_rG^\circ+RT\ln\left(\alpha_{M,\mathrm{eq}}^a\alpha_{X,\mathrm{eq}}^b\right)\]
\[\ln\left(\alpha_{M,\mathrm{eq}}^a\alpha_{X,\mathrm{eq}}^b\right)=-\frac{\Delta_rG^\circ}{RT}\]

Exponentiate both sides. At a specified temperature, pressure and standard-state convention, the right-hand side is fixed, so define it as the solubility product constant:

\[\boxed{K_{sp}=\alpha_{M,\mathrm{eq}}^a\alpha_{X,\mathrm{eq}}^b=\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta_rG^\circ}{RT}\right)}\]

The standard Gibbs energy combines the standard enthalpy and entropy of dissolution:

\[\Delta_rG^\circ=\Delta_rH^\circ-T\Delta_rS^\circ,\qquad \ln K_{sp}=-\frac{\Delta_rH^\circ}{RT}+\frac{\Delta_rS^\circ}{R}\]

Lattice disruption, solvent interactions and entropy all contribute to where equilibrium lies. The first law alone does not determine the product expression or the equilibrium composition.

05Recover the tutorial’s concentration formula

For a dissolved ion, a concentration-based activity can be written using an activity coefficient \(\gamma_i\):

\[\alpha_i=\gamma_i\frac{[i]}{c^\circ}\]

In the dilute ideal approximation, \(\gamma_i\approx1\). Substitute this into the thermodynamic expression:

\[K_{sp}\approx\left(\frac{[M]_{\mathrm{eq}}}{c^\circ}\right)^a\left(\frac{[X]_{\mathrm{eq}}}{c^\circ}\right)^b\]

When concentrations are entered numerically in mol L⁻¹ and \(c^\circ=1\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\), this becomes the familiar school shorthand:

\[\boxed{K_{sp}=[M]_{\mathrm{eq}}^a[X]_{\mathrm{eq}}^b\quad\text{(school concentration convention)}}\]

The rigorous thermodynamic Ksp is dimensionless. A concentration product written without the standard-concentration factors is an approximate convention. At appreciable ionic strength, activity coefficients matter and the simple concentration product need not remain constant.

Use free-ion concentrations. Metal tied up in a complex, or an anion converted into another acid–base form, cannot simply be counted as free M or X.

06Work through calcium fluoride

\[\ce{CaF2(s) <=> Ca^2+(aq) + 2F-(aq)}\]

The coefficient of fluoride is two, so its chemical-potential contribution is doubled. That multiplier becomes an exponent:

\[\Delta_rG=\mu_{\ce{Ca^2+}}+2\mu_{\ce{F-}}-\mu_{\ce{CaF2(s)}}\]
\[K_{sp}=\alpha_{\ce{Ca^2+}}\alpha_{\ce{F-}}^2\approx\left(\frac{[\ce{Ca^2+}]}{c^\circ}\right)\left(\frac{[\ce{F-}]}{c^\circ}\right)^2\]

The exponent follows the number of ions produced, not the magnitude of the ion’s charge. Calcium has charge +2 but exponent one; fluoride has charge −1 but exponent two.

A numerical check

Use a hypothetical model value \(K_{sp}=4.0\times10^{-11}\). Assume initially ion-free water, dilute ideal behaviour and no important side reactions. If the molar solubility is s:

\[[\ce{Ca^2+}]=s,\qquad[\ce{F-}]=2s\]
\[K_{sp}\approx\frac{s}{c^\circ}\left(\frac{2s}{c^\circ}\right)^2=4\left(\frac{s}{c^\circ}\right)^3\]
\[s=c^\circ\left(\frac{4.0\times10^{-11}}{4}\right)^{1/3}\approx2.15\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\]

Therefore \([\ce{F-}]\approx4.31\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\). The displayed concentrations keep an extra digit for the check.

Back-substitute: \((2.15\times10^{-4})(4.31\times10^{-4})^2\approx4.0\times10^{-11}\), recovering the model Ksp.

A common ion changes the relation to s. With initial fluoride concentration C, the school expression becomes \(K_{sp}\approx s(C+2s)^2\) using numerical molar concentrations. The thermodynamic constant remains fixed at the same temperature; the equilibrium solubility changes.

07What does “mixing thermodynamics” mean?

Mixing thermodynamics describes how mixing changes entropy, enthalpy and Gibbs free energy. Imagine two ideal gases separated by a partition. Once it is removed, each species can occupy the whole container. The mixed state has many more possible microscopic arrangements.

More accessible arrangements mean higher entropy. For an ideal mixture at the same temperature, mixing has no enthalpy change, so its positive entropy change lowers Gibbs free energy:

\[\Delta H_{\mathrm{mix}}=0,\qquad\Delta S_{\mathrm{mix}}>0,\qquad\Delta G_{\mathrm{mix}}=-T\Delta S_{\mathrm{mix}}<0\]

Dissolved ions spreading among solvent molecules also have a mixing contribution. Salt dissolution additionally involves a crystal lattice and ion–solvent interactions. Ideal mixing is the model that reveals the logarithmic term; it is not the entire energy balance for dissolving a real solid.

08See why mixing produces a logarithm

For two ideal components, let \(n_A,n_B\) be their amounts and \(x_A=n_A/(n_A+n_B)\), \(x_B=n_B/(n_A+n_B)\) their mole fractions. The entropy of mixing is:

\[\Delta S_{\mathrm{mix}}=-R\left(n_A\ln x_A+n_B\ln x_B\right)\]

For a genuine mixture, each mole fraction lies between zero and one. Its logarithm is negative, so the minus sign makes the entropy change positive. With zero ideal mixing enthalpy:

\[\Delta G_{\mathrm{mix}}=RT\left(n_A\ln x_A+n_B\ln x_B\right)\]

Add this contribution to the pure-component Gibbs energies, denoted by a star:

\[G=n_A\mu_A^*+n_B\mu_B^*+RT\left(n_A\ln x_A+n_B\ln x_B\right)\]

Now differentiate with respect to the amount of A. Remember that adding A changes both mole fractions. The result is:

\[\boxed{\mu_A=\mu_A^*+RT\ln x_A}\]
Show the derivative, including the cancellation

Let \(n=n_A+n_B\). Rewrite the mixing term first:

\[\frac{\Delta G_{\mathrm{mix}}}{RT}=n_A\ln n_A+n_B\ln n_B-n\ln n\]

Holding \(n_B\) fixed gives:

\[\frac{\partial(\Delta G_{\mathrm{mix}})}{\partial n_A}=RT\left[(\ln n_A+1)-(\ln n+1)\right]=RT\ln\frac{n_A}{n}=RT\ln x_A\]

The +1 terms cancel. The pure-component term adds \(\mu_A^*\), yielding the chemical potential above. Intermediate amount logarithms are understood with a common reference unit; the final logarithm is a dimensionless ratio.

Mix one mole of each ideal component

For \(n_A=n_B=1.00\ \mathrm{mol}\), the mole fractions are both 0.500:

\[\Delta S_{\mathrm{mix}}=-R\left[1.00\ln0.500+1.00\ln0.500\right]=2R\ln2\approx11.5\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}\]
\[\Delta G_{\mathrm{mix}}=-(298\ \mathrm{K})(2R\ln2)\approx-3.43\ \mathrm{kJ}\]

Check the sign and units: entropy increases; multiplying by −T gives a negative energy change. Mixing remains favourable in this ideal model.

For a dilute solute, mole fraction is proportional to concentration when the solvent dominates. Absorbing the fixed reference factor into a solute standard chemical potential gives the concentration-based form:

\[\mu_i\approx\mu_i^\circ+RT\ln\left(\frac{[i]}{c^\circ}\right)\]

Real ions interact, so use activities for the general expression \(\mu_i=\mu_i^\circ+RT\ln\alpha_i\). The second law supplies the equilibrium condition; the mixing model, together with the activity framework, supplies the composition dependence.

09Watch the driving force change with concentration

Combine \(\Delta_rG^\circ=-RT\ln K_{sp}\) with the current-composition expression. This makes the physical meaning especially clear:

\[\boxed{\Delta_rG=RT\ln\left(\frac{Q}{K_{sp}}\right)}\]
Dissolution driving force at 298 K
Current ion productDissolution ΔᵣG at 298 KThermodynamic tendency

As dissolution raises the free-ion activities, Q rises and the logarithmic term becomes larger. With enough solid available, the solution reaches saturation at Q = Ksp. If all solid dissolves first, the final solution can remain unsaturated.

At equilibrium there is no net dissolution: dissolution and crystallization can continue at equal rates. Thermodynamics predicts the direction and equilibrium composition; kinetic barriers determine how quickly they are reached. A supersaturated solution can persist if crystallization is slow.

The concentration product is the mathematical result of adding the ions’ chemical potentials and requiring zero reaction Gibbs-energy change at equilibrium.

10A second route: count microscopic states directly

The powers of concentration can be obtained by comparing the microscopic possibilities before and after one formula unit dissolves. This route uses statistical weights and factorials, without first introducing Gibbs energy or chemical potential.

Consider the same balanced reaction, with ion charges suppressed in the generic notation:

\[\ce{M_{a}X_{b}(s) <=> aM(aq) + bX(aq)}\]

Which microstates are counted? Equilibrium concerns the crystal, ions, solvent and surroundings together. The positions of the dissolved ions supply the concentration dependence. Lattice binding, hydration, internal motions and the surroundings’ energy states supply the remaining microscopic factors. These contributions determine the actual value of Ksp.

Step 1 · Label each possible amount dissolved

Let m be an integer counting the number of formula units that have dissolved, rather than a number of moles. If the initial solution contains NM,0 and NX,0 ions, then:

\[N_M=N_{M,0}+am,\qquad N_X=N_{X,0}+bm\]

Assume a large, dilute solution of volume V, at fixed temperature and specified pressure, with a bulk pure solid remaining. Treat the dissolution volume change as negligible and neglect interactions between solute ions. Solvent effects are included in concentration-independent single-ion factors in this ideal-dilute model.

At fixed total energy, accessible microstates of the complete isolated system are given equal probability. Counting how many of those states correspond to each m gives its probability. When the surroundings are represented by a thermal reservoir, the same calculation becomes an energy-weighted sum over states. Write Wm for this effective statistical weight; only ratios of the weights matter.

Step 2 · Count the dilute ions and account for identical particles

A freely dispersed ion can explore the solution volume, so its single-particle statistical weight is proportional to V. For N identical ions of one species, independent single-particle factors multiply, and division by N! removes duplicate labels of the same physical arrangement. The resulting ideal-dilute species factor has the form qN/N!, where q is the single-ion statistical weight.

To keep every factor dimensionless, introduce a reference-volume count L:

\[L\equiv N_Ac^\circ V,\qquad\frac{N_i}{L}=\frac{[i]}{c^\circ}\]

Here NA is Avogadro’s constant, c° = 1 mol L⁻¹, and V is in litres. L is a dimensionless normalization, not an actual number of lattice sites. The model’s physical microscopic length scales and energy sums are absorbed into the remaining statistical factors.

After collecting those factors, the composition-dependent weight can be written:

\[\boxed{W_m=C\,\frac{\eta^m L^{(a+b)m}}{N_M!\,N_X!}}\]

C is independent of m. The dimensionless factor η accounts for the microscopic statistical change per formula unit from the solid, hydration and other internal or reservoir contributions at the specified conditions. The volume factors and factorials display the composition dependence explicitly. The model assumes η is independent of the ion concentrations over the dilute range considered.

A bulk pure crystal supplies a fixed statistical weight ratio for removing one formula unit at fixed conditions. This is why the amount of remaining solid does not appear as another population factor in the concentration product. Surface effects and very small crystals can require corrections; the present model treats the macroscopic pure phase.

Where does the factor η come from?

Write the effective single-ion factors as qM = ζML and qX = ζXL. Let Zs(Ns) be the bulk crystal’s statistical weight after its other degrees of freedom have been summed. In the macroscopic pure-phase limit, its weight ratio for removing one formula unit is independent of the amount of solid:

\[\frac{Z_s(N_s-1)}{Z_s(N_s)}\simeq\frac1{\zeta_s}\]

Neglecting surface contributions and using the ideal-dilute factorization gives:

\[W_m\propto Z_s(N_{s,0}-m)\,\frac{(\zeta_ML)^{N_M}}{N_M!}\,\frac{(\zeta_XL)^{N_X}}{N_X!}\]

Substitute NM = NM,0 + am and NX = NX,0 + bm. All factors independent of m go into C; the remaining factor per dissolution event is:

\[\eta=\frac{\zeta_M^a\zeta_X^b}{\zeta_s}\]

These ζ factors are effective microscopic state sums with a common, consistent energy reference. The solvent and reservoir configurations have already been summed into them. A real aqueous-ion calculation needs the appropriate solvation model; a gas-phase translational partition function alone cannot supply it.

Step 3 · Compare neighbouring amounts dissolved

One more dissolution event adds a ions of M and b ions of X. Divide the new weight by the old one. The factorial ratios give a particularly transparent result:

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{W_{m+1}}{W_m}&=\eta L^{a+b}\frac{N_M!}{(N_M+a)!}\frac{N_X!}{(N_X+b)!}\\&=\frac{\eta L^{a+b}}{\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{a}(N_M+j)\prod_{k=1}^{b}(N_X+k)}.\end{aligned}\]

This ratio is exact for the displayed factorized model. The factorial terms show why adding ions becomes statistically less favourable as more identical ions are already present. When both populations are macroscopic, each N + small integer can be replaced by N:

\[\frac{W_{m+1}}{W_m}\simeq\frac{\eta L^{a+b}}{N_M^aN_X^b}=\frac{\eta}{([M]/c^\circ)^a([X]/c^\circ)^b}\]

This is the origin of the exponents: adding a indistinguishable M ions introduces a factors involving NM; adding b X ions introduces b factors involving NX. They become powers in the large-population limit.

Step 4 · Locate the most probable composition

If Wm+1/Wm > 1, the next amount dissolved is more probable. If it is < 1, the next amount is less probable. At an interior maximum, the ratios cross one. For large particle numbers the equilibrium distribution is sharply peaked, and neighbouring weights near the maximum have approximately equal ratios:

\[\frac{W_{m+1}}{W_m}\simeq1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\boxed{\left(\frac{[M]_{\mathrm{eq}}}{c^\circ}\right)^a\left(\frac{[X]_{\mathrm{eq}}}{c^\circ}\right)^b=\eta\equiv K_{sp}}\]

The equilibrium ion product follows from maximizing the statistical weight. No Gibbs-energy formula was needed to reach this result. Using numerical molar concentrations with c° = 1 mol L⁻¹ gives the familiar classroom form Ksp = [M]a[X]b.

Finite populations and phase boundaries. At a discrete maximum the ratios on either side bracket one; neither has to be exactly one. The concentration-product equality is the macroscopic approximation here. Also, if all the solid disappears before the interior maximum is reached, the solution can remain unsaturated. The available material limits the allowed m values.

Step 5 · See the square for calcium fluoride

\[\ce{CaF2(s) <=> Ca^2+(aq) + 2F-(aq)}\]
\[\frac{W_{m+1}}{W_m}=\frac{\eta L^3}{(N_{\ce{Ca^2+}}+1)(N_{\ce{F-}}+1)(N_{\ce{F-}}+2)}\]

The two extra fluoride ions create two fluoride population factors. For large populations, their product is approximately the square of the existing fluoride population. At the weight maximum:

\[\boxed{K_{sp}\simeq\frac{[\ce{Ca^2+}]_{\mathrm{eq}}}{c^\circ}\left(\frac{[\ce{F-}]_{\mathrm{eq}}}{c^\circ}\right)^2}\]

Adding a common ion raises its population and increases the denominator of the weight ratio at a given m. The most probable amount dissolved moves downward. This is the same common-ion effect derived directly from the counting factors.

A small-number check of the factorial reasoning

Use an illustrative calcium-fluoride-type model with initially zero ions and ηL³ = 30. Then NCa = m and NF = 2m. The exact neighbouring-weight ratio is:

\[\frac{W_{m+1}}{W_m}=\frac{30}{(m+1)(2m+1)(2m+2)}\]
ChangeWeight ratioConsequence
m = 0 → 130/(1 × 1 × 2) = 15The weight rises.
m = 1 → 230/(2 × 3 × 4) = 1.25The weight rises again.
m = 2 → 330/(3 × 5 × 6) = 1/3The weight falls.

Check: the most probable integer is m = 2, assuming enough solid is available. Treating m as continuous and using the large-population approximation gives 4m³ = 30, or m ≈ 1.96. The close numerical result is illustrative; real macroscopic solutions have vastly larger populations, while small systems retain fluctuations and finite-count corrections. These supplied toy weights are not a prediction of calcium fluoride’s measured Ksp.

Step 6 · What counts alone explain, and where microscopic energies enter

The volume and identical-particle factors explain the product and its stoichiometric powers. Predicting η also requires the states and energies of the crystal, solvent and ions. In a simplified model with one effective energy cost Δε per formula unit and an internal-state multiplicity ratio g, the statistical factor might have the form:

\[\eta=g\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta\varepsilon}{k_BT}\right)\]

This is a toy single-energy model; realistic factors are sums over many microscopic states. A positive energy cost reduces their thermal weight, while more accessible internal states can increase it. In a complete isolated-system description, the reservoir’s corresponding state counts produce the same weighting. At fixed pressure the accounting includes the associated volume work.

Thus a microscopic derivation can account for the entire equilibrium, including energetics, through complete state counting. Counting only pictures of the dissolved ions supplies the concentration dependence and leaves η unspecified.

At finite ionic strength the ideal independent-ion factorization fails. Correlations then require activity corrections; the thermodynamic product uses αMaαXb, as in sections 04–05. Those corrections need an interaction model, beyond the simple factorial calculation.

Connect the result to the thermodynamic route after deriving it

Both routes describe the same equilibrium. The state-counting model identifies its standard microscopic factor with the ideal-dilute Ksp. The earlier thermodynamic route gives:

\[\eta=K_{sp}=\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta_rG^\circ}{RT}\right)\]

Here ΔᵣG° is molar, so the denominator uses R. In the toy microscopic energy model, Δε is per formula unit and the denominator uses kB. These are different accounting levels of the same physical equilibrium. Read the entropy lesson’s two starting points for how state counting and macroscopic thermodynamics connect.

The concentration powers come from the factorial changes caused by adding the stoichiometric numbers of identical ions. Equilibrium selects the composition with the largest complete microscopic statistical weight.
