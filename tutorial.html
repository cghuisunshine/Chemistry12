Start with three questions

Ask what particles are present, what conservation rule connects their amounts, and whether the calculation describes a rate, an equilibrium, or a measured signal. Name the species and the stage of the process before choosing a formula. “Initial after mixing” and “final at equilibrium” are different states.

Follow the definitions: amount & reaction extent → internal energy & enthalpy → entropy → Gibbs energy → chemical potential → activity → Q & K. For speed, follow rate & activation; for ions, follow charge & electrical work.

1. Amount, concentration, and reaction extent

Amount n is measured in moles: one mole contains exactly 6.02214076 × 10²³ specified entities. Molar concentration c = n/V uses solution volume, not solvent volume. Square brackets [X] mean the molar concentration of the particular species X. An analytical concentration C counts how much solute was introduced per litre, across all the forms it becomes. For a monoprotic acid with no other acid-containing species, C = [HA] + [A⁻]. Molar mass is the mass per mole of the specified substance, commonly in g mol⁻¹. Uppercase M after a number means mol L⁻¹; a symbol for molar mass must be read from context.

Stoichiometric coefficients give mole ratios. A subscript gives the composition of one formula unit. Changing a subscript changes the substance; multiplying a reaction changes the amount of the same chemical process. Reactions conserve each element and total electric charge.

n = cV; mass = n × molar mass
1 L = 1000 mL; 1 A = 1 C s⁻¹; 1 V = 1 J C⁻¹
T(K) = temperature(°C) + 273.15

The book defines reaction extent ξ as an amount in moles that measures progress for the equation as written. Assign each species a signed stoichiometric number νᵢ: negative for a reactant and positive for a product. Then nᵢ = nᵢ,₀ + νᵢξ. For N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃, an advance of 0.10 mol consumes 0.10 mol N₂ and 0.30 mol H₂ and forms 0.20 mol NH₃. ξ is neither elapsed time nor the number of moles of every species. At fixed volume, x = ξ/V turns this definition into an ICE table.

Source: §7.1(a), printed p. 201 (PDF 233): extent and reaction Gibbs energy.

2. Internal energy, heat, work, and enthalpy

Internal energy U accounts for molecular kinetic and potential energy within the system. Heat q and work w describe transfers across its boundary. With the book’s sign convention, energy entering is positive and ΔU = q + w. Heat transfer arises from a temperature difference; expansion against an external pressure does work w = −∫pext dV. A sample stores internal energy; q describes how energy crossed its boundary along a particular process.

Enthalpy is defined by H = U + pV. At constant pressure with only pressure–volume work and mechanical equilibrium at the endpoints, ΔH = qp. This makes enthalpy useful for reactions in a flask open to the atmosphere: it includes the energy needed to accommodate volume change. Exothermic means ΔH < 0 under those conditions. H is a state function, so its change depends on endpoints even when a catalyst changes the route.

Sources: §§2.1–2.2, printed pp. 30–33 (PDF 62–65): U, heat, work, and the First Law; §2.5(a), printed pp. 40–41 (PDF 72–73): H and constant-pressure heat.

3. Entropy: a state function with a microscopic meaning

The thermodynamic definition is dS = δqrev/T: find an entropy change by imagining a reversible path between the states and adding its heat transfers divided by absolute temperature. The subscript matters: an irreversible process does not generally satisfy ΔS = qactual/T. For example, an isolated gas expanding freely has q = 0 but gains entropy.

The microscopic connection is S = kB ln W, where W counts microstates compatible with the specified macroscopic state. A microstate specifies a microscopic arrangement and distribution of energy. “More disordered” can be a first image, but counting accessible possibilities is more precise. Mixing can increase W; hydration can constrain nearby solvent molecules. Consequently, dissolving does not guarantee a positive entropy change of the solution. The Second Law requires the entropy of system plus surroundings to increase in a spontaneous irreversible change.

Source: §3.2, printed pp. 78–81 (PDF 110–113), eqs. 3.1 and 3.5: thermodynamic and statistical entropy.

4. Gibbs energy translates the Second Law into a chemical criterion

G = H − TS combines properties of the system so that, at fixed temperature and pressure, a spontaneous change with no imposed non-expansion work lowers G. For heat exchange with a reservoir at T and only pressure–volume work, ΔSsurroundings = −ΔHsystem/T. Thus ΔStotal = ΔSsystem − ΔHsystem/T = −ΔGsystem/T. The sign of ΔG therefore incorporates the surroundings’ entropy change too.

An endothermic change can be favourable if TΔS outweighs ΔH. A negative ΔG describes a thermodynamic tendency under the stated constraints; the pathway still determines the rate. For a reversible process at constant T and p, the maximum non-expansion work delivered to the surroundings is −ΔG. This is the bridge to cell voltage.

Source: §3.5, printed pp. 95–99 (PDF 127–131): Gibbs energy, spontaneity, and useful work.

5. Chemical potential: explain “per mole” carefully

The book defines μᵢ = (∂G/∂nᵢ)T,p,other amounts, the partial molar Gibbs energy. Imagine adding a tiny amount dnᵢ while holding temperature, pressure, and every other species’ amount fixed: μᵢ tells how much the mixture’s G changes per added mole at that composition. “Partial” means that the surroundings of the added particles matter. μᵢ is generally different in different mixtures; it is not simply the Gibbs energy of an isolated molecule.

At fixed T and p: dG = Σᵢ μᵢ dnᵢ
With dnᵢ = νᵢ dξ: dG = (Σᵢ νᵢμᵢ) dξ
ΔᵣG = (∂G/∂ξ) at fixed T,p = Σᵢ νᵢμᵢ

Σ means “add over all species.” For A ⇌ B this becomes ΔᵣG = μB − μA. For a general reaction it is the stoichiometrically weighted sum that vanishes at equilibrium; the chemical potentials of all different species need not be equal. ΔᵣG has units J mol⁻¹ of reaction and is a local slope. A finite system change ΔG is the integral ∫ΔᵣG dξ as composition changes. Equilibrium means a minimum in G along the allowed reaction progress, not G = 0.

Sources: §5.1(b–c), printed pp. 138–139 (PDF 170–171), eqs. 5.4–5.7; §7.1(a), printed p. 201 (PDF 233), eqs. 7.1–7.3.

6. Activity expresses the composition dependence of chemical potential

μᵢ = μᵢ° + RT ln aᵢ
aᵢ = exp[(μᵢ − μᵢ°)/(RT)]
Concentration scale: aᵢ = γᵢcᵢ/c°

This relation defines activity relative to a chosen standard chemical potential μᵢ°. Activity is dimensionless; γᵢ measures the departure from the chosen ideal model. At 298 K, multiplying activity by ten raises μᵢ by RT ln 10 ≈ 5.71 kJ mol⁻¹. This is why a concentration change can alter a reaction’s direction without changing its standard reaction Gibbs energy. The logarithmic composition term has a mixing-entropy basis: ideal mixing can lower G even when its enthalpy change is zero.

Why a logarithm? For a pure substance, dμ = Vmdp at fixed temperature. For an ideal gas Vm = RT/p, so integrating from p° to p gives μ − μ° = ∫RT dp/p = RT ln(p/p°). In an ideal gas mixture the species contributes its partial pressure pᵢ. The ideal-solution expression similarly contains RT ln xᵢ, with xᵢ the mole fraction. Real-fluid activity retains this useful logarithmic form while accounting for interactions through γᵢ. It is defined through chemical potential, not simply by counting particles.

On the concentration scale c° = 1 mol L⁻¹. In sufficiently dilute solutions γᵢ is often near one, so a species’ numerical molarity can approximate its dimensionless activity. For an ideal gas aᵢ = pᵢ/p°, with p° = 1 bar; real gas behaviour needs a fugacity correction. Pure solids and pure liquids in their standard states have activity one. In dilute water solutions, the solvent mole fraction is close to one, so water activity is usually approximated as one too. Omitting water becomes less accurate in concentrated mixtures.

The thermodynamic K and Q are dimensionless activity products. School Kc and Ksp expressions often leave standard-concentration factors implicit. Use the course data booklet’s conventions for calculations. Do not take logarithms of a dimensional quantity literally: pH ≈ −log₁₀([H₃O⁺]/c°) explains the familiar shorthand. At appreciable ionic strength, free-ion concentrations and activities can differ.

Sources: §3.9(c), printed pp. 108–109 (PDF 140–141): pressure dependence of Gibbs energy; §5.2(b), printed p. 143 (PDF 175): entropy of ideal mixing; §5.7(c), printed p. 161 (PDF 193): activity and standard states; §5.9, printed pp. 163–165 (PDF 195–197): ion activities. The book often uses molality b/b°; concentration-scale activities use their corresponding μᵢ° and standard states. Numerical constants must match that convention.

7. Build Q and K from those definitions

Insert μᵢ = μᵢ° + RT ln aᵢ into ΔᵣG = Σνᵢμᵢ. Because ν ln a = ln(aᵛ) and sums of logarithms become logarithms of products, define Q = ∏aᵢνᵢ. The symbol ∏ means “multiply over all species.” Negative reactant ν values put their activities in the denominator; positive product ν values put them in the numerator. The powers follow from stoichiometric progress and chemical potential, even for a multistep reaction.

ΔᵣG = ΔᵣG° + RT ln Q = RT ln(Q/K)
ΔᵣG° = −RT ln K
R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; use J mol⁻¹ with this R

Negative ΔᵣG favours forward conversion, zero means reaction equilibrium, and positive favours reverse conversion. At equilibrium the reaction Gibbs energy is zero; the system’s total G is not zero. An activation barrier controls accessibility and speed. A favourable reaction can remain slow, while a catalyst cannot change the equilibrium target at fixed temperature.

The symbol ° specifies standard states at the temperature of interest; it does not mean 25 °C. For specified standard states, K = exp(−ΔᵣG°/RT), whereas Q describes the actual composition. A reaction at equilibrium usually has ΔᵣG° ≠ 0. Doubling an equation doubles ΔᵣG° and squares K; reversing it changes the sign of ΔᵣG° and gives 1/K.

Source: §7.2, printed pp. 205–206 (PDF 237–238), Justification 7.1 and eqs. 7.14–7.17.

8. Rate, reaction coordinate, and activation describe the pathway

Reaction rate r = (1/V)dξ/dt measures progress per volume per time. A rate law describes how r depends on composition; its exponents are experimental reaction orders. An elementary step is one microscopic event; its molecularity counts the reacting entities. These are separate definitions. A mechanism must reproduce the observed law, but matching one law does not uniquely prove a mechanism.

The reaction coordinate describes molecular motions such as changing bond lengths and angles. It differs from the bulk reaction extent ξ. A transition state lies at a barrier on a pathway. The measured Arrhenius activation energy is defined more generally by Eₐ = RT² d(ln k)/dT. A simple barrier picture explains many positive Eₐ values; complex mechanisms can have other temperature dependences. Activation Gibbs energy Δ‡G describes the enthalpy and entropy cost of forming a transition-state configuration, while ΔᵣG describes the reaction’s thermodynamic driving force.

Sources: §22.2, printed pp. 794–796 (PDF 826–828): rate and order; §§22.5–22.6, printed pp. 807–810 (PDF 839–842): Eₐ and elementary steps; §24.5(a), printed pp. 883–884 (PDF 915–916): activation thermodynamics.

9. Charge balance and energy per charge

A macroscopic bulk electrolyte is essentially electrically neutral: Σzᵢcᵢ = 0, where zᵢ is signed ionic charge. Use every ion, including spectators. This charge balance is independent of atom balance and equilibrium; it often supplies the missing equation in a pH calculation. Local charge separation at an electrode interface can still create an electrical potential difference.

Current I = dQcharge/dt measures charge flow. One mole of electrons carries charge of magnitude F = NAe ≈ 96485 C mol⁻¹. A potential difference of one volt corresponds to one joule per coulomb. For a reversible cell transferring z moles of electrons per mole of reaction, the maximum electrical work gives ΔᵣG = −zFE. Thus voltage connects the same Gibbs-energy slope to a measurable electrical quantity. Here z counts transferred electrons; zᵢ above denotes signed ion charge.

Sources: §5.9, printed pp. 163–164 (PDF 195–196): electrically neutral electrolytes; §7.7(a), printed pp. 219–220 (PDF 251–252): Faraday constant and reversible electrical work.

1.1 Collision theory & reaction rate

A reaction needs particles to collide with enough energy to overcome the activation energy, Eₐ, and with a suitable orientation. Increasing the number of effective collisions per second increases the rate.

Measure rate using a change in concentration, gas volume, or mass divided by elapsed time. A reactant concentration falls, so place a minus sign before its slope when reporting a positive disappearance rate. An average rate is a secant slope over an interval; an instantaneous rate is the slope of a tangent.

For aA → bB: rate = −(1/a) Δ[A]/Δt = (1/b) Δ[B]/Δt

Concentration rates commonly use mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹. The coefficients relate rates of consumption and production; they do not generally give the exponents in an experimental rate law.

Build the result from the definitions

  1. Define progress using one species. The balanced equation gives dnᵢ = νᵢdξ. Rearranging means dξ = dnᵢ/νᵢ. Each species therefore counts the same progress after its stoichiometric factor is removed.
  2. Introduce the measuring volume and time. At fixed V, nᵢ = V[i], so dnᵢ/dt = Vd[i]/dt. Substitute into the rate definition:
    r = (1/V)(1/νᵢ)dnᵢ/dt = (1/νᵢ)d[i]/dt
    For aA → bB, νA = −a and νB = +b, producing the familiar minus sign for A.
  3. Translate another measured signal. If a gas product B is collected at constant p and T and treated as ideal, nB = pVgas/(RT). Thus dnB/dt = [p/(RT)]dVgas/dt. Divide by b and by the reaction-mixture volume to obtain r. Gas collection volume and reacting-solution volume have different jobs.

Interpret the result. For 2A → 3B, a disappearance slope −d[A]/dt = 0.020 M s⁻¹ corresponds to r = 0.010 M s⁻¹ and d[B]/dt = 0.030 M s⁻¹. The three values describe the same conversion using different counts.

Conditions to check. Use a closed, well-mixed system with a single independent net reaction for this simple normalization. A changing solution volume, a gas leak, or changing gas temperature can alter the signal without the assumed chemical progress.

Definitions to revisit: extent ξ → reaction rate.

Begin with the book’s amount relation dnᵢ = νᵢdξ. Dividing by dt defines progress per second; dividing by a fixed volume gives r = (1/V)dξ/dt. Substituting for dξ gives r = (1/νᵢ)d[i]/dt. This explains both the coefficient and the sign in a rate expression. The coefficient normalizes different species’ slopes to the same reaction as written.

A derivative means the limiting slope as the time interval becomes small. The integrated first-order law below adds these small changes over time; it requires a particular measured rate law, beyond the definition of rate. At changing volume, concentration also changes through dilution:

d[i]/dt = (νᵢ/V)dξ/dt − ([i]/V)dV/dt

If water is added without reaction, ξ stays constant while [i] falls. Ask what was measured before interpreting a slope as chemical conversion. Collision theory adds a possible molecular explanation for conversion; for solutions the encounter may involve diffusion and solvent rearrangement.

Textbook basis: Atkins & de Paula, Physical Chemistry, 8th ed., §22.2(a), printed pp. 794–795 (PDF 826–827), eqs. 22.2–22.3: definition of rate; §24.1, printed p. 870 (PDF 902): scope of simple collision theory. Explanations and classroom applications here are adapted in original wording.

Why the minus sign and coefficient?

For 2A → 3B at constant volume, let Δξ be the amount of reaction in moles. Then Δn(A) = −2Δξ and Δn(B) = 3Δξ. Dividing by volume and time gives one common rate:

r = Δξ/(VΔt) = −½ Δ[A]/Δt = ⅓ Δ[B]/Δt

If A falls by 0.020 M in 10 s, its disappearance rate is 0.0020 M s⁻¹, the reaction rate is 0.0010 M s⁻¹, and B forms at 0.0030 M s⁻¹. “Disappearance rate” and “normalized reaction rate” therefore need different numbers. A tangent gives the limit as the measuring interval becomes very short.

Why must collisions have energy and orientation?

Atoms must approach closely enough for electrons and bonds to rearrange. An unfavourable approach can separate particles again without making the required bonds. The collision picture is a useful molecular model; it is not a claim that every reaction is one collision between two molecules.

From a rate law to an amount remaining

[A]₀ is the initial concentration; [A] is the concentration at time t; k has units s⁻¹ for this first-order disappearance law.

  1. State the model. For A → P with negligible reverse reaction, each A has the same chance per unit time of reacting. Thus −d[A]/dt = k[A].
  2. Separate the variables and add the small changes.
    d[A]/[A] = −k dt
    ∫ from [A]₀ to [A] d[A]/[A] = −k ∫ from 0 to t dt
    ln([A]/[A]₀) = −kt
    [A] = [A]₀ exp(−kt)
    The logarithm compares two concentrations, so its argument is dimensionless.
  3. Set the amount to half its initial value.
    ln(½) = −kt½ → t½ = ln 2/k
    The same fraction disappears in each equal time interval. The amount disappearing per second gets smaller.

Check: if k = 0.0200 s⁻¹, t½ = 34.7 s. After 69.3 s, [A]/[A]₀ = 0.250. A plot of ln([A]/[A]₀) against t has slope −k.

When this works: Constant volume and constant k; a measured first-order law, with reverse reaction negligible. This is an optional extension beyond average-rate calculations.

1.2 Factors that change rate

Temperature: raising temperature increases the fraction of collisions with energy at least Eₐ. Concentration: more dissolved reactant particles usually create more opportunities for collisions. Increasing a reacting gas’s partial pressure by compression has a similar effect.

Surface area: crushing a solid exposes more reacting sites. Nature of reactants: bond strengths and physical state affect how readily particles react. Catalysts: supply an alternative mechanism with a lower activation barrier and are regenerated overall. Inhibitors slow a reaction through mechanisms such as blocking active sites.

Common trap A catalyst changes the pathway and speed, but does not change ΔH, the equilibrium constant, or the final equilibrium composition at a fixed temperature.

Why the two-temperature Arrhenius equation works

A is the prefactor (same units as k); Eₐ is activation energy; R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; T is in kelvin.

  2. Write it at both temperatures, then subtract.
    ln k₂ − ln k₁ = (ln A − Eₐ/RT₂) − (ln A − Eₐ/RT₁)
    ln(k₂/k₁) = (Eₐ/R)(1/T₁ − 1/T₂)
    The unknown prefactor cancels; subtraction of logarithms becomes a logarithm of a ratio.
  3. Exponentiate to find a multiplier.
    k₂/k₁ = exp[(Eₐ/R)(1/T₁ − 1/T₂)]
    For positive Eₐ and T₂ > T₁, the exponent is positive, so k increases.

Try the rate model: for Eₐ = 50.0 kJ mol⁻¹, warming from 298 to 308 K gives an exponent of 0.655 and k₂/k₁ = 1.93. Convert 50.0 kJ to 50,000 J before using R.

1.3 Energy diagrams & enthalpy

On a potential-energy diagram, the vertical axis represents energy and the horizontal axis represents reaction progress, not time. The peak is a transition state. The forward activation energy is the peak energy minus the reactant energy.

ΔH = H(products) − H(reactants)
Eₐ(reverse) = Eₐ(forward) − ΔH

An exothermic reaction has ΔH < 0 and releases energy; an endothermic reaction has ΔH > 0 and absorbs energy. Reversing a thermochemical equation reverses ΔH. Multiplying the equation multiplies ΔH by the same factor. If energy is quoted per mole, use the amount corresponding to the equation’s stated basis.

Say to students“First account for energy entering as heat and leaving as expansion work. Enthalpy combines the terms so that its change gives the heat at constant pressure. Then the diagram can distinguish the reaction’s energy change from the barrier along its route.”

How does enthalpy arise? Start with the First Law

Chemists ordinarily use a property known as enthalpy (H) to describe the thermodynamics of chemical and physical processes. Enthalpy is defined as the sum of a system’s internal energy (U) and the mathematical product of its pressure (P) and volume (V):

H = U + PV

Enthalpy is also a state function. Enthalpy values for specific substances cannot be measured directly; only enthalpy changes for chemical or physical processes can be determined. For processes that take place at constant pressure (a common condition for many chemical and physical changes), the enthalpy change (ΔH) is:

ΔH = ΔU + PΔV

The mathematical product PΔV represents work (w), namely, expansion or pressure-volume work as noted. By their definitions, the arithmetic signs of ΔV and w will always be opposite:

PΔV = −w

Substituting this equation and the definition of internal energy into the enthalpy-change equation yields:

ΔH = ΔU + PΔV
= qp + w − w
= qp

where qp is the heat of reaction under conditions of constant pressure.

Excerpt: OpenStax Chemistry 2e, §5.3: Enthalpy. Here uppercase P denotes the same pressure written as lowercase p elsewhere on this page.

Keep the conditions: ΔH = qp requires constant pressure and only pressure–volume work. At fixed volume with no other work, qV = ΔU.

Original energy-budget example: where did the heat go?

Imagine a closed gas sample absorbing 500 J while expanding by 2.00 L against a constant pressure of 1.00 × 10⁵ Pa. Take its initial and final pressures to equal that external pressure. Since 2.00 L = 0.00200 m³ and 1 Pa m³ = 1 J:

w = −(1.00 × 10⁵ Pa)(0.00200 m³) = −200 J
ΔU = +500 J − 200 J = +300 J
ΔH = +300 J + 200 J = +500 J = qp

The sample retains 300 J as increased internal energy and transfers 200 J outward as expansion work. Adding pΔV restores that work contribution when calculating the heat received. Enthalpy is defined by this combination of state properties; energy conservation supplies the reason its change equals constant-pressure heat under these conditions.

Connect this to the diagram. For specified initial and final states, ΔH = Hproducts − Hreactants. A negative value means the reacting system releases heat under the conditions above; a positive value means it absorbs heat. The intermediate barrier concerns the pathway. These are the endpoint and pathway questions compared below.

Why releasing heat and reacting spontaneously differ

U is internal energy; q is heat entering the system; w is work on it; S is entropy; G = H − TS.

  1. Account for expansion work. With only pressure–volume work at constant pressure, ΔU = q − pΔV. From H = U + pV:

    A negative ΔH means heat leaves the reacting system.

1.4 Reaction mechanisms

A mechanism breaks an overall reaction into elementary steps. Add the steps and cancel species that appear on both sides to recover the overall equation.

An intermediate is produced in one step and consumed later. A catalyst is consumed in an early step and regenerated later. Neither appears in the net equation. A multistep energy profile has multiple peaks, with intermediates at the valleys.

The slow, rate-determining step constrains the overall rate. Its activation barrier is measured from the energy of the species entering that step, rather than from an arbitrary common baseline. A mechanism must agree with both the balanced reaction and experimental evidence.

2.1 A dynamic balance

In a closed system, a reversible reaction can reach dynamic equilibrium: forward and reverse reaction rates become equal. Particles keep reacting, but macroscopic concentrations stay constant.

Equal rates do not require equal concentrations. A system may contain far more products than reactants at equilibrium. On a rate–time graph, the curves meet; on a concentration–time graph, curves level off at potentially different heights.

For aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD:
Kc = [C]ᶜ[D]ᵈ / ([A]ᵃ[B]ᵇ)

Use equilibrium concentrations and coefficients from the balanced equation. Omit pure solids and pure liquids because their activities are effectively constant. In school calculations, concentrations approximate activities; thermodynamic K is dimensionless.

For an elementary A ⇌ B reaction, r forward = kf[A] and r reverse = kr[B]. Equating rates gives [B]/[A] = kf/kr = K. The concentrations need not be equal because the rate constants need not be equal. This is a useful example, not a way to infer arbitrary overall rate laws from balanced equations.

Where the powers in Q and K come from

μᵢ is a species’ chemical potential (molar Gibbs energy in the mixture); aᵢ is dimensionless activity; ξ counts reaction progress in moles.

  1. Count one mole of reaction as written. For 2A ⇌ B, advancing by dξ consumes 2dξ of A and produces dξ of B. At fixed T and p:
    dG = μA(−2 dξ) + μB(dξ)
    ΔᵣG = dG/dξ = μB − 2μA
    The reaction Gibbs energy is the slope of total G against reaction progress.
  2. Insert composition dependence.
    μᵢ = μᵢ° + RT ln aᵢ
    ΔᵣG = (μB° − 2μA°) + RT(ln aB − 2 ln aA)
    = ΔᵣG° + RT ln(aB/aA²)
    Logarithm rules turn the coefficient 2 into a power. Thus Q = aB/aA², even if the reaction has many elementary steps.
  3. Find the minimum of G. At an interior equilibrium, the slope is zero and Q has its equilibrium value K:
    0 = ΔᵣG° + RT ln K
    ΔᵣG° = −RT ln K; K = exp(−ΔᵣG°/RT)
    G itself is not zero. The forward and reverse molecular processes still occur.

General rule: for aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, Q = aCᶜaDᵈ/(aAᵃaBᵇ). Pure solid activity is one while that phase remains; the solid is omitted because multiplying by one changes nothing.

When this works: Fixed T and p. Use activities for the thermodynamic expression; replace them by normalized concentrations only within the dilute-solution approximation.

2.2 Q, K & reaction direction

The reaction quotient, Q, uses the same expression as K but with the concentrations at the moment being considered.

Q < K
Net forward reaction; products form.

Q = K
Equilibrium; no net reaction.

Q > K
Net reverse reaction; reactants form.

A very large K indicates products are strongly favoured at equilibrium; a very small K indicates reactants are favoured. Neither tells you how quickly equilibrium will be reached. Only a temperature change changes K for a specified reaction. Reversing the equation gives 1/K; multiplying every coefficient by n gives Kⁿ.

2.3 Le Chatelier’s principle & the Haber process

A disturbed equilibrium responds with a net reaction that partly counteracts the disturbance. Adding a reactant or removing a product usually favours the forward direction. The response does not completely undo the initial change.

Compressing a gas mixture at constant temperature favours the side with fewer moles of gas. Equal gas coefficients mean no shift from volume change. Adding an inert gas at constant volume changes neither reacting partial pressures nor equilibrium composition; adding it at constant total pressure can change the volume and shift an equilibrium.

N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g) + heat

For ammonia synthesis, compression and cooling favour ammonia. Industrial conditions balance equilibrium yield with reaction speed. A catalyst speeds the approach to equilibrium. Heating favours the endothermic direction; for this exothermic forward reaction, heating decreases K.

Derive the effect of temperature on K

ΔᵣH° is standard reaction enthalpy, not an activation barrier. T₁ and T₂ are absolute temperatures.

  1. Start with equilibrium thermodynamics.
    ln K = −ΔᵣG°/(RT)

    d(ΔᵣG°/T)/dT = −ΔᵣH°/T²

    ln(K₂/K₁) = (ΔᵣH°/R) ∫ from T₁ to T₂ T⁻² dT

3.1 Solutions, ions & net ionic equations

An ionic solid can dissociate into hydrated ions when it dissolves. A molecular solute may remain as molecules; some molecular substances, such as acids, react with water to produce ions. Mobile ions allow a solution to conduct electricity.

A saturated solution is in equilibrium with undissolved solute at a specified temperature. Dissolving and crystallizing occur at equal rates. Solubility is the amount that dissolves to form a saturated solution, and can be reported in mol L⁻¹ or g L⁻¹.

For precipitation reactions, use the solubility table to predict products. Write a balanced molecular equation, split soluble strong electrolytes into ions, then cancel spectator ions to obtain the net ionic equation. Do not split solids, liquids, gases, or weak electrolytes into ions.

For a substance moving between two phases, equilibrium requires equal chemical potentials for that substance in the phases. An ionic solid transfers an electrically neutral formula unit as several ions, so the condition is stoichiometrically weighted: for AgCl, μAg⁺ + μCl⁻ = μAgCl(s). Separating the lattice and hydrating the separated ions are useful contributions in a thermodynamic cycle. Their enthalpy changes combine with entropy changes to determine the dissolution Gibbs energy. Initially low ion activities can make dissolution favourable; as ions accumulate, RT ln a raises their contributions until the weighted chemical-potential difference vanishes at saturation.

Water orientation around ions gives a molecular picture of hydration, but it can restrict solvent configurations as well as disperse solute. That is why “more particles means more entropy” is incomplete. Spectator ions cancel from the net reaction’s stoichiometric sum because their ν values are zero; they can still alter the environment and therefore the active ions’ activity coefficients.

Why can water separate ions?

Water is polar: its oxygen end attracts cations and its hydrogen ends attract anions. Separating a lattice costs energy; hydration releases energy. Mixing also changes entropy. Their combined Gibbs-energy change, rather than lattice strength alone, determines the equilibrium extent of dissolution. A molecular substance such as sugar can dissolve as neutral molecules, so dissolving is not synonymous with producing ions.

Saturation and electrical conductivity

A saturated solution has the equilibrium dissolved composition at the specified temperature and conditions. Excess solid provides a visible phase with which it can equilibrate, but a solution can be exactly saturated without visible solid. If solid is present, dissolution and crystallization rates balance. Conductivity requires mobile charged species; a dissolved sugar solution does not conduct like an ionic solution of comparable concentration.

Why spectator ions cancel

AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq) represents Ag⁺, NO₃⁻, Na⁺, and Cl⁻ in water. Na⁺ and NO₃⁻ occur unchanged on both sides. Cancelling them leaves Ag⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl(s), conserving both atoms and total charge. Keep weak electrolytes intact because they are not almost entirely free ions.

A supersaturated solution has more dissolved solute than its equilibrium value and may persist until nucleation occurs. In a net ionic equation, “spectator” means no net chemical conversion; those ions can still affect activity coefficients and conductivity.

3.2 Ksp & molar solubility

The solubility product, Ksp, describes an ionic solid’s dissolution equilibrium. The solid is omitted from the expression.

CaF₂(s) ⇌ Ca²⁺(aq) + 2F⁻(aq)
Ksp = [Ca²⁺][F⁻]²

If the molar solubility in initially ion-free water is s, the ion concentrations are s and 2s, so Ksp = s(2s)² = 4s³. The relation between s and ion concentrations depends on stoichiometry.

To convert molar solubility to mass concentration, multiply by the solid’s molar mass. A smaller Ksp does not always mean a smaller molar solubility when comparing salts with different ion ratios.

3.4 Common ions, pH & hard water

A common ion supplied by another dissolved compound usually lowers a salt’s molar solubility. Write equilibrium concentrations that include both the added ions and those from the dissolving salt.

Acid can increase the solubility of salts containing basic anions, such as carbonate: H₃O⁺ consumes the anion, encouraging further dissolution. This is not a universal effect for every salt. Complex formation can also favour dissolution by reducing the free metal-ion concentration.

4.1 Acids, bases & conjugate pairs

A Brønsted–Lowry acid donates a proton, H⁺; a Brønsted–Lowry base accepts one. In water, H⁺ is commonly represented as H₃O⁺. A conjugate acid–base pair differs by exactly one proton and one unit of charge.

  1. Track what leaves with the hydrogen. A proton carries +1 charge but no electron. When HA donates H⁺, the bonding pair remains with A, making the conjugate species one charge unit more negative. Thus HSO₄⁻ gives SO₄²⁻, and NH₄⁺ gives NH₃; neither rule depends on a neutral starting acid.
  2. Write two reactions with a common proton carrier.
    HA + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + A⁻ Ka(HA)
    BH⁺ + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + B Ka(BH⁺)
    Reverse the second equation and add. Hydronium and water cancel, leaving the direct proton-transfer equation.
  3. Convert reaction addition into a constant. Reversal reciprocates the second constant. Therefore Ktransfer = Ka(HA)/Ka(BH⁺). Replacing each Ka by 10−pKa gives:
    Ktransfer = 10pKa(BH⁺) − pKa(HA)
    A positive pKa difference gives a product-favoured transfer equilibrium.

Interpret the result. Removing one H⁺ from H₂CO₃ produces HCO₃⁻. Removing another produces CO₃²⁻ in a second equilibrium. H₂CO₃ and CO₃²⁻ therefore cannot be the immediate pair used in a single KaKb relation.

Conditions to check. Compare constants in the same solvent, at the same temperature and standard states. Initial amounts still determine the final composition. A species can play an acid role in one reaction and a base role in another.

Back to the textbook: proton transfer is an equilibrium between two conjugate pairs

Definitions to revisit: species and charge conservation → solvated species → adding reactions.

The Brønsted definition identifies the reaction roles: an acid donates a proton and a base accepts one. It tells which species are conjugate partners; it does not by itself say how far proton transfer proceeds. Apply the book’s Gibbs-energy and activity framework to answer that next question.

Write HA + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + A⁻, then reverse BH⁺ + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + B. Adding cancels hydronium and water to give HA + B ⇌ A⁻ + BH⁺. Standard Gibbs energies add, so the transfer constant is Ka(HA)/Ka(BH⁺). The weaker-acid rule follows from this quotient under the same solvent, temperature, and standard states.

For illustration, pKa(HA) = 4 and pKa(BH⁺) = 9 give Ktransfer = 10⁵. Products are favoured in the equilibrium quotient; the actual final amounts still depend on initial amounts. H⁺(aq) refers to a solvated proton, represented here by H₃O⁺. Bonding and solvation influence chemical potentials, so comparing gas-phase bond strengths alone cannot fully rank aqueous acids.

Charge is a useful check

Removing H⁺ leaves the electron pair behind, so the conjugate base has one fewer H and a charge one unit more negative. H₂CO₃ gives HCO₃⁻; NH₄⁺ gives NH₃. Adding H⁺ reverses this rule. NH₃ accepts H⁺ using its nitrogen lone pair. Water can accept a proton to become H₃O⁺ or donate one to become OH⁻: it is amphiprotic. HCO₃⁻ likewise has two possible roles.

Derive the weaker-acid/weaker-base rule

For HA + B ⇌ A⁻ + BH⁺, combine the ionization of HA with the reverse ionization of BH⁺. Their constants divide:

Ktransfer = Ka(HA)/Ka(BH⁺)
= 10^[pKa(BH⁺) − pKa(HA)]

A larger pKa for BH⁺ means it is the weaker acid, so proton transfer to B is favoured. This compares acids in the same solvent at the same temperature.

University connection & limits

H⁺(aq) is shorthand for a solvated proton; water forms a fluctuating hydrogen-bonded network, not a reservoir of bare protons. H₃O⁺ is the useful school representation. The Lewis definition is broader: a base donates an electron pair and an acid accepts one, so acid–base chemistry can occur without proton transfer.

4.2 Strength, concentration & water

Strong describes essentially complete ionization or dissociation under the stated aqueous conditions. Concentrated describes a large amount of solute per volume. A weak acid can be concentrated; a strong acid can be dilute.

2H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + OH⁻
Kw = [H₃O⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C

Neutral water has equal H₃O⁺ and OH⁻ concentrations at any temperature. At 25 °C these are 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ M. Because Kw changes with temperature, neutral pH is not always 7.

Common trap Do not assume every hydrogen atom in an acid formula ionizes completely. For example, the second ionization of sulfuric acid requires its own equilibrium treatment when relevant.

Why water appears on both sides of acid–base chemistry

One water molecule can donate a proton to another. The autoionization equilibrium has a small product of hydronium and hydroxide activities. Treating water’s solvent activity as one gives the classroom equation:

Kw ≈ [H₃O⁺][OH⁻]

Why an acid formula does not give all its hydronium at once

4.3 The logarithmic pH scale
pH = −log₁₀[H₃O⁺] · pOH = −log₁₀[OH⁻]
[H₃O⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ · pH + pOH = pKw
At 25 °C: pKw = 14.00

These classroom equations use molar concentration as an approximation to activity in dilute solution. Each pH unit corresponds to a tenfold change in hydronium concentration. Lower pH means greater hydronium concentration.

A pH outside 0–14 is possible; 0 and 14 are not absolute limits. Very concentrated solutions need activity corrections, while extremely dilute strong acids need water’s own ionization included.

For logarithms, the number of decimal places in pH corresponds to the number of significant figures in the concentration. Keep guard digits during calculations and round at the end.

4.4 Strong-acid/base mixtures & titration

For a mixture, do stoichiometry before pH. Calculate moles of acid and base, apply the balanced neutralization ratio, find the excess, and divide by total solution volume. Then calculate pH or pOH.

H₃O⁺ + OH⁻ → 2H₂O
n = cV, with V in litres

In a titration, a standard solution of known concentration reacts with the analyte. At the equivalence point, amounts match the balanced reaction’s stoichiometry. An indicator’s observed endpoint should be close to equivalence. They are different concepts.

To standardize a solution, titrate it against a suitable accurately known standard. Rinse the burette with titrant, remove tip bubbles, read the meniscus consistently, and use concordant titres. Follow your teacher’s lab-safety instructions.

5.1 Weak acids & Ka

A weak acid only partly transfers protons to water. Its equilibrium constant Ka quantifies its acid strength at a specified temperature.

HA + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + A⁻
Ka = [H₃O⁺][A⁻]/[HA]

For an initially pure weak acid of concentration C, let x be the amount ionized, neglecting water’s contribution when justified. Then [H₃O⁺] = [A⁻] = x and [HA] = C − x, giving Ka = x²/(C − x).

If x is small relative to C, x ≈ √(KaC). Check 100x/C ≤ 5% after solving. Otherwise use the quadratic. Percent ionization is 100x/C; dilution increases percent ionization even though hydronium concentration usually decreases.

5.2 Weak bases, conjugates & hydrolysis
B + H₂O ⇌ BH⁺ + OH⁻
Kb = [BH⁺][OH⁻]/[B]
Ka(BH⁺) × Kb(B) = Kw

For a weak base, solve for [OH⁻], then find pOH and pH. KaKb = Kw applies to a conjugate pair at the same temperature, not an arbitrary acid and base.

Hydrolysis is an ion’s acid–base reaction with water. Acetate produces some OH⁻ and makes a solution basic; ammonium produces some H₃O⁺ and makes it acidic. Ions from strong acids and strong bases, such as Na⁺ and Cl⁻, have negligible hydrolysis in the usual school model. Small, highly charged hydrated metal ions can acidify water.

For salts with both acidic and basic ions, compare the relevant Ka and Kb and account for competing equilibria if needed.

What salt hydrolysis means

5.3 Buffers & indicators

A buffer contains appreciable amounts of a weak acid and its conjugate base, or a weak base and its conjugate acid. The base member consumes added acid; the acid member consumes added base.

pH ≈ pKa + log₁₀([A⁻]/[HA])
pKa = −log₁₀Ka

This Henderson–Hasselbalch form is useful when both buffer components remain appreciable and concentration ratios approximate activity ratios. A buffer works best around pH = pKa, typically within about one pH unit. Greater amounts of its components provide greater capacity.

After adding a strong acid or base, first update the buffer component moles by neutralization, then use the new ratio. If one component is exhausted, the buffer equation is no longer suitable.

An indicator is a weak acid/base whose forms have different colours. Choose one whose transition range lies within the steep pH change near the expected equivalence point.

Follow every algebra step in the buffer equation

pH = −log₁₀ a(H₃O⁺); pKa = −log₁₀ Ka. HA and A⁻ are the two buffer partners.

  1. Isolate hydrogen-ion activity.
    Ka = a(H₃O⁺)a(A⁻)/a(HA)
    a(H₃O⁺) = Ka × a(HA)/a(A⁻)
  2. Take the negative base-ten logarithm.
    pH = −log₁₀ Ka − log₁₀[a(HA)/a(A⁻)]
    = pKa + log₁₀[a(A⁻)/a(HA)]
    ≈ pKa + log₁₀([A⁻]/[HA])
    The plus sign comes from inverting the ratio. More A⁻ therefore raises pH.

    pH ≈ 4.76 + log₁₀(0.0900/0.110) = 4.67
    The common final volume cancels from the concentration ratio.

5.4 Titration curves, anhydrides & acid rain

Strong-acid/strong-base equivalence is approximately pH 7 at 25 °C. Weak-acid/strong-base equivalence is basic because the conjugate base hydrolyzes. Weak-base/strong-acid equivalence is acidic.

At half-equivalence in a weak-acid/strong-base titration, equal amounts of HA and A⁻ remain, so pH ≈ pKa. Before equivalence, use buffer chemistry when applicable; at equivalence, use hydrolysis; beyond equivalence, use excess strong titrant.

6.1 Oxidation, reduction & oxidation numbers

Oxidation is loss of electrons; reduction is gain of electrons. Both happen together. An oxidizing agent accepts electrons and is reduced; a reducing agent donates electrons and is oxidized.

An element in its elemental form has oxidation number 0. A monatomic ion’s number equals its charge. Oxidation numbers sum to the total charge of a species. Oxygen is usually −2, except in cases such as peroxides; hydrogen is usually +1, except in metal hydrides.

Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ (oxidation)
Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu (reduction)

An increase in oxidation number signals oxidation. Here Zn is the reducing agent, and Cu²⁺ is the oxidizing agent.

The book defines oxidation as removal of electrons, reduction as addition, and a redox couple as the oxidized and reduced species connected by a half-reaction, written Ox + ze⁻ → Red. This specifies the reacting species and electron count. An oxidation number is a formal assignment used to find that count, rather than a measurement of an atom’s actual charge in a molecule.

Why oxidation number rises when electrons are lost

In Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻, zinc’s assigned number changes from 0 to +2, consistent with losing two negatively charged electrons. Cu²⁺ → Cu changes +2 to 0 by gaining two. An oxidation number is the formal charge assigned if bonding electrons are given to the more electronegative atom. In covalent molecules it need not equal a measured atomic charge or a Lewis formal charge.

Use the charge sum to determine unknown numbers

For MnO₄⁻, let Mn be x. Then x + 4(−2) = −1, so x = +7. Reducing it to Mn²⁺ changes Mn by five units and requires five electrons per Mn. 6.2 Balance redox reactions

Separate the oxidation and reduction half-reactions. In acidic solution:

  1. Balance all atoms except O and H.
  2. Balance oxygen using H₂O, then hydrogen using H⁺.
  3. Balance charge using electrons.
  4. Multiply half-reactions to equalize electron transfer; add and cancel.

For basic solution, balance as if acidic, then add enough OH⁻ to both sides to convert every H⁺ into H₂O. Cancel excess water. Check every element and total charge.

In redox titrations, use the balanced equation to convert titrant moles to analyte moles. Electron transfer determines the mole ratio; it is not automatically 1:1.

6.3 Cells, standard potentials & spontaneity

A galvanic cell uses a spontaneous redox reaction to produce electrical energy. Oxidation occurs at the anode and reduction at the cathode. Electrons move through the external circuit from anode to cathode. The salt bridge maintains electrical neutrality by ion migration, rather than carrying electrons.

E°cell = E°red(cathode) − E°red(anode)
ΔG° = −nFE°cell

Use the tabulated reduction potentials in the subtraction formula. A positive E°cell indicates thermodynamic favourability for the reaction as written under standard conditions. It does not guarantee a fast reaction. Standard aqueous conditions use unit activity, often approximated by 1 M, and standard gas pressure is 1 bar; specify temperature, commonly 25 °C.

Multiplying a half-reaction does not multiply its electrode potential. For competing reactions, compare feasible oxidation and reduction pairs; actual conditions, kinetics, and overpotential can affect what is observed.

Explain the electrode rules with the zinc–copper cell

Zinc oxidation makes electrons available at the anode; copper-ion reduction consumes them at the cathode. The external wire carries electrons. In the salt bridge, anions generally migrate toward the zinc compartment as Zn²⁺ forms, and cations toward the copper compartment as Cu²⁺ is consumed. Derive Nernst from the same equilibrium energy equation

n is electrons transferred per balanced cell reaction (dimensionless here); F = 96485 C mol⁻¹. E is reversible cell voltage.

  1. Convert chemical driving force to energy per charge. One mole of cell reaction transfers nF coulombs. The maximum electrical work delivered is nFE per mole of reaction:
    ΔᵣG = −nFE; ΔᵣG° = −nFE°
    The minus sign makes a favourable ΔᵣG < 0 correspond to positive E.
  2. Substitute into the Gibbs-energy equation.
    ΔᵣG = ΔᵣG° + RT ln Q
    −nFE = −nFE° + RT ln Q
    E = E° − (RT/nF) ln Q
    At 298.15 K, ln Q = 2.303 log₁₀Q, giving E = E° − (0.05916 V/n) log₁₀Q.

    n = 2; Q = a(Zn²⁺)/a(Cu²⁺)
    If Q = 100: E = 1.10 − (0.05916/2)log₁₀(100)

    Changing composition changes actual voltage without changing E° at fixed T.

6.4 Electrolysis, plating & corrosion

An electrolytic cell uses an external power supply to drive a nonspontaneous reaction. Oxidation is still at the anode and reduction at the cathode, but the anode is positive and cathode negative, opposite to a galvanic cell’s signs.

In metal electroplating, the object is the cathode, where dissolved metal ions gain electrons and deposit. In aqueous electrolysis, consider water as a possible reactant alongside the ions.

Q = It · n(e⁻) = Q/F
F ≈ 96 485 C mol⁻¹ e⁻
n(metal) = n(e⁻)/z

Here I is current in amperes, t is time in seconds, and z is electrons per deposited metal atom. This ideal calculation assumes 100% current efficiency.

Iron corrosion couples metal oxidation with a reduction reaction, often involving oxygen and water. Coatings block exposure; sacrificial protection uses a more readily oxidized metal, such as zinc, to protect the iron.

Use E°red(Cu²⁺/Cu) = +0.34 V and E°red(Zn²⁺/Zn) = −0.76 V.

Reveal worked solution
  1. Cu²⁺ has the more positive reduction potential and is reduced at the cathode. Zn is oxidized at the anode.
  2. Overall: Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s).
  3. E°cell = 0.34 − (−0.76) = +1.10 V. Electrons flow from zinc to copper through the wire.
Worked example 6.2

Calculate copper electroplating

A current of 2.00 A flows for 30.0 min through Cu²⁺ solution. Use M(Cu) = 63.5 g mol⁻¹ and assume 100% current efficiency.

Reveal worked solution
  1. t = 1800 s; Q = It = 2.00 × 1800 = 3600 C.
  2. n(e⁻) = 3600/96485 = 0.0373 mol. Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu, so n(Cu) = 0.0187 mol.
  3. m(Cu) = (3600/96485)/2 × 63.5 = 1.18 g, using unrounded intermediate values.
