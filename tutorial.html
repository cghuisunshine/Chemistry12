Shared foundationsSymbols, units, activities, and energy: explain these once

Start with three questions

Ask what particles are present, what conservation rule connects their amounts, and whether the calculation describes a rate, an equilibrium, or a measured signal. Name the species and the stage of the process before choosing a formula. “Initial after mixing” and “final at equilibrium” are different states.

Amount, concentration, and balanced equations

Amount n is measured in moles: one mole contains exactly 6.02214076 × 10²³ specified entities. Molar concentration c = n/V uses solution volume, not solvent volume. Square brackets [X] mean the molar concentration of the particular species X. An analytical concentration C counts how much solute was introduced per litre, across all the forms it becomes. For a monoprotic acid with no other acid-containing species, C = [HA] + [A⁻]. Molar mass is the mass per mole of the specified substance, commonly in g mol⁻¹. Uppercase M after a number means mol L⁻¹; a symbol for molar mass must be read from context.

Stoichiometric coefficients give mole ratios. A subscript gives the composition of one formula unit. Changing a subscript changes the substance; multiplying a reaction changes the amount of the same chemical process. Reactions conserve each element and total electric charge.

n = cV; mass = n × molar mass
1 L = 1000 mL; 1 A = 1 C s⁻¹; 1 V = 1 J C⁻¹
T(K) = temperature(°C) + 273.15

Why university chemistry uses activity

Concentration measures the amount per volume. Activity measures how that species contributes to chemical potential relative to a chosen standard state. On a concentration scale, aᵢ = γᵢcᵢ/c°, where γᵢ is an activity coefficient and c° = 1 mol L⁻¹. In sufficiently dilute solutions γᵢ is often near one, so a species’ numerical molarity can approximate its dimensionless activity. For an ideal gas aᵢ = pᵢ/p°, with p° = 1 bar. Real gas behaviour needs a fugacity correction. Pure solids and pure liquids in their standard states have activity one; dilute aqueous water is usually approximated this way as solvent.

The thermodynamic K and Q are dimensionless activity products. School Kc and Ksp expressions often leave standard-concentration factors implicit. Use the course data booklet’s conventions for calculations. Do not take logarithms of a dimensional quantity literally: pH ≈ −log₁₀([H₃O⁺]/c°) explains the familiar shorthand. At appreciable ionic strength, free-ion concentrations and activities can differ.

Rate, heat, and thermodynamic direction

Rate measures how fast conversion occurs. Enthalpy H describes the heat change at constant pressure under the stated work assumptions. Gibbs energy G = H − TS includes entropy S; entropy reflects accessible microscopic arrangements and energy dispersal. At fixed temperature and pressure, the reaction Gibbs energy ΔᵣG predicts the favourable net direction at the current composition. The symbol ° specifies standard states, not a mandatory temperature. Δ means a difference; ln is the natural logarithm; log₁₀ is the base-ten logarithm.

ΔᵣG = ΔᵣG° + RT ln Q = RT ln(Q/K)
ΔᵣG° = −RT ln K
R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; use J mol⁻¹ with this R

Negative ΔᵣG favours forward conversion, zero means reaction equilibrium, and positive favours reverse conversion. At equilibrium the reaction Gibbs energy is zero; the system’s total G is not zero. An activation barrier controls accessibility and speed. A favourable reaction can remain slow, while a catalyst cannot change the equilibrium target at fixed temperature.

Textbook foundation: Atkins & de Paula, §5.7, printed p. 161 (PDF 193): activity and chemical potential; §7.2, printed p. 205 (PDF 237): Gibbs energy and the reaction quotient. The book commonly uses molality b/b°; this tutorial uses the matching concentration-scale convention c/c° for school calculations, with its corresponding standard chemical potential.

Kinetics

How fast does a reaction happen?

Why does food spoil faster on a warm day?

1.1 Collision theory & reaction rate

A reaction needs particles to collide with enough energy to overcome the activation energy, Eₐ, and with a suitable orientation. Increasing the number of effective collisions per second increases the rate.

Measure rate using a change in concentration, gas volume, or mass divided by elapsed time. A reactant concentration falls, so place a minus sign before its slope when reporting a positive disappearance rate. An average rate is a secant slope over an interval; an instantaneous rate is the slope of a tangent.

For aA → bB: rate = −(1/a) Δ[A]/Δt = (1/b) Δ[B]/Δt

Concentration rates commonly use mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹. The coefficients relate rates of consumption and production; they do not generally give the exponents in an experimental rate law.

Follow the derivation: From a rate law to an amount remaining →

Tutor explanation 1.1What a rate actually measures

Say to students“A balanced equation gives a recipe. Rate tells us how quickly that recipe is being carried out. Count the same reaction using any species, then divide by its recipe coefficient.”

Why the minus sign and coefficient?

For 2A → 3B at constant volume, let Δξ be the amount of reaction in moles. Then Δn(A) = −2Δξ and Δn(B) = 3Δξ. Dividing by volume and time gives one common rate:

r = Δξ/(VΔt) = −½ Δ[A]/Δt = ⅓ Δ[B]/Δt

If A falls by 0.020 M in 10 s, its disappearance rate is 0.0020 M s⁻¹, the reaction rate is 0.0010 M s⁻¹, and B forms at 0.0030 M s⁻¹. “Disappearance rate” and “normalized reaction rate” therefore need different numbers. A tangent gives the limit as the measuring interval becomes very short.

Why must collisions have energy and orientation?

Atoms must approach closely enough for electrons and bonds to rearrange. An unfavourable approach can separate particles again without making the required bonds. The collision picture is a useful molecular model; it is not a claim that every reaction is one collision between two molecules.

Physical chemistry · follow the reasoning

From a rate law to an amount remaining

[A]₀ is the initial concentration; [A] is the concentration at time t; k has units s⁻¹ for this first-order disappearance law.

  1. State the model. For A → P with negligible reverse reaction, each A has the same chance per unit time of reacting. Thus −d[A]/dt = k[A].
  2. Separate the variables and add the small changes.
    d[A]/[A] = −k dt
    ∫ from [A]₀ to [A] d[A]/[A] = −k ∫ from 0 to t dt
    ln([A]/[A]₀) = −kt
    [A] = [A]₀ exp(−kt)
    The logarithm compares two concentrations, so its argument is dimensionless.
  3. Set the amount to half its initial value.
    ln(½) = −kt½ → t½ = ln 2/k
    The same fraction disappears in each equal time interval. The amount disappearing per second gets smaller.

Check: if k = 0.0200 s⁻¹, t½ = 34.7 s. After 69.3 s, [A]/[A]₀ = 0.250. A plot of ln([A]/[A]₀) against t has slope −k.

When this works: Constant volume and constant k; a measured first-order law, with reverse reaction negligible. This is an optional extension beyond average-rate calculations.

Adapted from Atkins & de Paula, Physical Chemistry, 8th ed., §22.3(a), eqs. 22.12. Read printed p. 799 (PDF page 831) ↗

University connection & limits

The derivative form is r = −(1/a)d[A]/dt. It assumes constant volume when interpreting concentration changes as chemical conversion; dilution alone also changes concentration. Gas-volume or mass-loss slopes must be converted through the gas law or molar mass before comparison with a molar reaction rate.

Ask the studentIf 2A → B consumes A at 0.060 M s⁻¹, how fast does B appear? Expected: 0.030 M s⁻¹; the reaction rate is also 0.030 M s⁻¹.

1.2 Factors that change rate

Temperature: raising temperature increases the fraction of collisions with energy at least Eₐ. Concentration: more dissolved reactant particles usually create more opportunities for collisions. Increasing a reacting gas’s partial pressure by compression has a similar effect.

Surface area: crushing a solid exposes more reacting sites. Nature of reactants: bond strengths and physical state affect how readily particles react. Catalysts: supply an alternative mechanism with a lower activation barrier and are regenerated overall. Inhibitors slow a reaction through mechanisms such as blocking active sites.

Common trap A catalyst changes the pathway and speed, but does not change ΔH, the equilibrium constant, or the final equilibrium composition at a fixed temperature.

Follow the derivation: Why the two-temperature Arrhenius equation works →

Tutor explanation 1.2Separate the rate law from the temperature effect

Say to students“Concentration changes how many opportunities particles have to react. Temperature changes the success probability. A catalyst changes the route through which success becomes possible.”

Rate law, rate constant, and reaction order

r = k[A]ᵐ[B]ⁿ

Here r is the reaction rate, k the rate constant at the stated conditions, and m and n experimentally determined orders. Doubling A multiplies the rate by 2ᵐ while B and temperature stay fixed. Order zero means no change; order one means twice the rate; order two means four times the rate. Overall balanced coefficients do not usually determine these orders. The units of k must make the full expression have rate units: for overall order m + n, they are M^(1 − m − n) s⁻¹ when rate is in M s⁻¹. More surface area exposes additional sites without changing the solid’s bulk concentration.

Physical chemistry · follow the reasoning

Why the two-temperature Arrhenius equation works

A is the prefactor (same units as k); Eₐ is activation energy; R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; T is in kelvin.

  1. Take a logarithm. From k = A exp(−Eₐ/RT), obtain ln k = ln A − Eₐ/(RT). A plot against 1/T has slope −Eₐ/R. Logarithms of k and A here use the same reference units.
  2. Write it at both temperatures, then subtract.
    ln k₂ − ln k₁ = (ln A − Eₐ/RT₂) − (ln A − Eₐ/RT₁)
    ln(k₂/k₁) = (Eₐ/R)(1/T₁ − 1/T₂)
    The unknown prefactor cancels; subtraction of logarithms becomes a logarithm of a ratio.
  3. Exponentiate to find a multiplier.
    k₂/k₁ = exp[(Eₐ/R)(1/T₁ − 1/T₂)]
    For positive Eₐ and T₂ > T₁, the exponent is positive, so k increases.

Try the rate model: for Eₐ = 50.0 kJ mol⁻¹, warming from 298 to 308 K gives an exponent of 0.655 and k₂/k₁ = 1.93. Convert 50.0 kJ to 50,000 J before using R.

When this works: A and Eₐ remain approximately constant over the interval. A rate multiplier equals this k multiplier only when the concentration factors are unchanged.

Adapted from Atkins & de Paula, Physical Chemistry, 8th ed., §22.5, eq. 22.29. Read printed p. 807 (PDF page 839) ↗

University connection & limits

Real catalysts can change the prefactor and several barriers. The slider deliberately keeps the prefactor fixed to isolate one effect. A catalyst accelerates both directions consistently with the same equilibrium constant; it does not necessarily multiply both rates by the same factor at every composition. An inhibitor might bind a catalyst or interrupt a chain mechanism.

Ask the studentIf [A] doubles and rate quadruples at fixed [B] and T, what is the order in A? Expected: two. Does k quadruple? No; the concentration factor changes.

1.3 Energy diagrams & enthalpy

On a potential-energy diagram, the vertical axis represents energy and the horizontal axis represents reaction progress, not time. The peak is a transition state. The forward activation energy is the peak energy minus the reactant energy.

ΔH = H(products) − H(reactants)
Eₐ(reverse) = Eₐ(forward) − ΔH

An exothermic reaction has ΔH < 0 and releases energy; an endothermic reaction has ΔH > 0 and absorbs energy. Reversing a thermochemical equation reverses ΔH. Multiplying the equation multiplies ΔH by the same factor. If energy is quoted per mole, use the amount corresponding to the equation’s stated basis.

Follow the derivation: Why releasing heat and reacting spontaneously differ →

Tutor explanation 1.3The barrier and the endpoint energy answer different questions

Say to students“The hill height tells us how difficult the route is. The difference between the starting and finishing heights tells us the overall energy change.”

Derive the reverse barrier from one common peak

Call the reactant, product, and transition-state energies Hᵣ, Hₚ, and H‡ on an enthalpy-style profile. Then Eₐ,f = H‡ − Hᵣ, Eₐ,r = H‡ − Hₚ, and ΔH = Hₚ − Hᵣ. Subtracting these expressions gives:

Eₐ,r = Eₐ,f − ΔH

For an exothermic reaction, ΔH is negative, so the reverse barrier is larger. Use one pathway and the same energy basis for all three heights. A multistep route needs each peak compared with its neighbouring valley.

Physical chemistry · follow the reasoning

Why releasing heat and reacting spontaneously differ

U is internal energy; q is heat entering the system; w is work on it; S is entropy; G = H − TS.

  1. Account for expansion work. With only pressure–volume work at constant pressure, ΔU = q − pΔV. From H = U + pV:
    ΔH = ΔU + pΔV = qₚ
    A negative ΔH means heat leaves the reacting system.
  2. Include the surroundings. For a constant-temperature reservoir, ΔSsurroundings = −ΔHsystem/T. Therefore:
    ΔStotal = ΔSsystem − ΔHsystem/T
    −TΔStotal = ΔHsystem − TΔSsystem = ΔGsystem
    A spontaneous change with no extracted non-expansion work increases total entropy, so ΔG < 0.
  3. Keep the barrier separate. ΔG compares a change in thermodynamic state. An activation barrier determines how readily the pathway can be followed. A catalyst alters that pathway while preserving the endpoint difference.

Illustrative reaction: ΔᵣH° = +20.0 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔᵣS° = +100 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ give ΔᵣG° = 20.0 − 298(0.100) = −9.80 kJ mol⁻¹ at 298 K. An endothermic forward reaction can be favourable at standard composition.

When this works: Constant temperature and pressure for the Gibbs criterion. The equality qₚ = ΔH additionally assumes only pressure–volume work. The example uses standard reaction quantities.

Adapted from Atkins & de Paula, Physical Chemistry, 8th ed., §2.5 and §3.5. Read printed p. 96 (PDF page 128) ↗

University connection & limits

A potential-energy diagram is a school approximation to a richer energy landscape. Arrhenius activation energy, activation enthalpy, and activation Gibbs energy are related but distinct quantities. Exothermic does not by itself mean spontaneous: at constant T and pressure, ΔG = ΔH − TΔS also includes entropy S, which describes the distribution of energy and accessible microscopic arrangements. A transition state is a fleeting configuration at a barrier, not an isolable intermediate.

Ask the studentA reaction has ΔH = +20 kJ mol⁻¹ and forward barrier 70 kJ mol⁻¹. Reverse barrier? Expected: 50 kJ mol⁻¹. Can a catalyst change +20 to −20? No.

1.4 Reaction mechanisms

A mechanism breaks an overall reaction into elementary steps. Add the steps and cancel species that appear on both sides to recover the overall equation.

An intermediate is produced in one step and consumed later. A catalyst is consumed in an early step and regenerated later. Neither appears in the net equation. A multistep energy profile has multiple peaks, with intermediates at the valleys.

The slow, rate-determining step constrains the overall rate. Its activation barrier is measured from the energy of the species entering that step, rather than from an arbitrary common baseline. A mechanism must agree with both the balanced reaction and experimental evidence.

Follow the derivation: Eliminate an intermediate to explain the observed rate →

Tutor explanation 1.4Why the mechanism matters more than the overall recipe for rate

Say to students“The net equation tells us the shopping list. The mechanism tells us which events must actually happen and in what order.”

Track an intermediate and derive an elementary rate law

Consider the illustrative steps A + B → I (slow), followed by I + B → P (fast). Adding them gives A + 2B → P. I is made and then used, so it is an intermediate. If the first step controls the rate under these conditions, r ≈ k[A][B], even though the overall equation contains 2B. For an elementary bimolecular step, two reacting particles must meet; that is why both concentrations enter its mass-action rate law.

Tell a catalyst from an intermediate

In A + C → I, then I → P + C, C is consumed and regenerated: it is the catalyst. I is formed and consumed: it is the intermediate. Both cancel, but their order of appearance differs. Valleys represent intermediates; peaks represent transition states.

Physical chemistry · follow the reasoning

Eliminate an intermediate to explain the observed rate

For A → I → P, k₁ and k₂ are first-order step constants; I is an intermediate.

  1. Write formation minus consumption.
    d[I]/dt = k₁[A] − k₂[I]
    A small intermediate population can still turn over rapidly.
  2. Use a steady-state approximation. After the initial buildup, if I is removed much faster than A supplies it (k₂ ≫ k₁), its concentration changes only slowly:
    d[I]/dt ≈ 0 → [I] ≈ (k₁/k₂)[A]
    This does not mean [I] = 0, or that the whole reaction is at equilibrium.
  3. Substitute into product formation.
    d[P]/dt = k₂[I] ≈ k₁[A]
    The observable rate follows the supply of I. This explains the first-step bottleneck rather than simply naming the slow step.

Distinction: in A ⇌ I → P, a fast pre-equilibrium would instead relate [I]/[A] to an equilibrium ratio. “Steady intermediate concentration” and “forward/reverse equilibrium” are different assumptions.

When this works: An irreversible consecutive first-order model with k₂ ≫ k₁, after the short initial transient. Other mechanisms need their own formation and consumption equations.

Adapted from Atkins & de Paula, Physical Chemistry, 8th ed., §22.7(b), eqs. 22.42–22.44. Read printed p. 812–813 (PDF page 844) ↗

University connection & limits

“The slowest step” is an introductory approximation. A later step’s rate also depends on how much of its reactant intermediate exists. A fast pre-equilibrium or steady-state treatment may be needed to express that concentration using observable reactants. The tallest absolute peak does not automatically identify the controlling step. Matching a measured rate law supports a mechanism but does not prove it uniquely.

Ask the studentFor the two-step example, why is the order in B one rather than two? Expected: one B is involved in the assumed controlling elementary step; the second is consumed later.

Dynamic equilibrium

When opposing processes balance.

Can a reaction keep happening even when nothing appears to change?

2.1 A dynamic balance

In a closed system, a reversible reaction can reach dynamic equilibrium: forward and reverse reaction rates become equal. Particles keep reacting, but macroscopic concentrations stay constant.

Equal rates do not require equal concentrations. A system may contain far more products than reactants at equilibrium. On a rate–time graph, the curves meet; on a concentration–time graph, curves level off at potentially different heights.

For aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD:
Kc = [C]ᶜ[D]ᵈ / ([A]ᵃ[B]ᵇ)

Use equilibrium concentrations and coefficients from the balanced equation. Omit pure solids and pure liquids because their activities are effectively constant. In school calculations, concentrations approximate activities; thermodynamic K is dimensionless.

Follow the derivation: Where the powers in Q and K come from →

Tutor explanation 2.1Equal rates, chemical activities, and the origin of exponents

Say to students“At equilibrium the two molecular processes continue equally quickly. A constant population does not mean the particles have stopped changing identity.”

A simple kinetic route to K

For an elementary A ⇌ B reaction, r forward = kf[A] and r reverse = kr[B]. Equating rates gives [B]/[A] = kf/kr = K. The concentrations need not be equal because the rate constants need not be equal. This is a useful example, not a way to infer arbitrary overall rate laws from balanced equations.

Physical chemistry · follow the reasoning

Where the powers in Q and K come from

μᵢ is a species’ chemical potential (molar Gibbs energy in the mixture); aᵢ is dimensionless activity; ξ counts reaction progress in moles.

  1. Count one mole of reaction as written. For 2A ⇌ B, advancing by dξ consumes 2dξ of A and produces dξ of B. At fixed T and p:
    dG = μA(−2 dξ) + μB(dξ)
    ΔᵣG = dG/dξ = μB − 2μA
    The reaction Gibbs energy is the slope of total G against reaction progress.
  2. Insert composition dependence.
    μᵢ = μᵢ° + RT ln aᵢ
    ΔᵣG = (μB° − 2μA°) + RT(ln aB − 2 ln aA)
    = ΔᵣG° + RT ln(aB/aA²)
    Logarithm rules turn the coefficient 2 into a power. Thus Q = aB/aA², even if the reaction has many elementary steps.
  3. Find the minimum of G. At an interior equilibrium, the slope is zero and Q has its equilibrium value K:
    0 = ΔᵣG° + RT ln K
    ΔᵣG° = −RT ln K; K = exp(−ΔᵣG°/RT)
    G itself is not zero. The forward and reverse molecular processes still occur.

General rule: for aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, Q = aCᶜaDᵈ/(aAᵃaBᵇ). Pure solid activity is one while that phase remains; the solid is omitted because multiplying by one changes nothing.

When this works: Fixed T and p. Use activities for the thermodynamic expression; replace them by normalized concentrations only within the dilute-solution approximation.

Adapted from Atkins & de Paula, Physical Chemistry, 8th ed., §7.2, Justification 7.1 and eqs. 7.14–7.17. Read printed p. 205–206 (PDF page 237) ↗

University connection & limits

Pure solids and pure liquids in their standard states have activity one, not concentration zero. Their amount can change while their activity stays essentially fixed, provided the phase remains. 2.2 Q, K & reaction direction

The reaction quotient, Q, uses the same expression as K but with the concentrations at the moment being considered.

Q < K
Net forward reaction; products form.

Q = K
Equilibrium; no net reaction.

Q > K
Net reverse reaction; reactants form.

A very large K indicates products are strongly favoured at equilibrium; a very small K indicates reactants are favoured. Neither tells you how quickly equilibrium will be reached. Only a temperature change changes K for a specified reaction. Reversing the equation gives 1/K; multiplying every coefficient by n gives Kⁿ.

Say to students“Q describes the mixture we have now. K describes the equilibrium target at this temperature. The comparison tells us which net change can lower Gibbs energy.”

Physical chemistry · follow the reasoning

Turn Q versus K into an energy prediction

Q describes the present composition; K describes equilibrium at the same temperature. Both are dimensionless.

  1. Substitute the equilibrium relation.
    ΔᵣG = ΔᵣG° + RT ln Q
    = −RT ln K + RT ln Q = RT ln(Q/K)
  2. Read the sign. If Q/K < 1, ln(Q/K) is negative: forward progress lowers G. If Q/K > 1, reverse progress lowers G. The target is Q/K = 1.
  3. Check the numerical scale.
    At 298 K, K = 4 and Q = 1:
    ΔᵣG = (8.314)(298) ln(¼) = −3435 J mol⁻¹
    ≈ −3.43 kJ per mole of reaction as written
    The net direction is forward. This energy does not give the rate.

Why K changes when an equation is doubled: doubling the coefficients doubles ΔᵣG°. Then Knew = exp(−2ΔᵣG°/RT) = K². For the reverse equation, ΔᵣG° changes sign and Knew = 1/K.

When this works: The reaction, temperature, and standard states must match. If a starting activity is zero, interpret the expression through its limiting value.

2.3 Le Chatelier’s principle & the Haber process

A disturbed equilibrium responds with a net reaction that partly counteracts the disturbance. Adding a reactant or removing a product usually favours the forward direction. The response does not completely undo the initial change.

Compressing a gas mixture at constant temperature favours the side with fewer moles of gas. Equal gas coefficients mean no shift from volume change. Adding an inert gas at constant volume changes neither reacting partial pressures nor equilibrium composition; adding it at constant total pressure can change the volume and shift an equilibrium.

N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g) + heat

For ammonia synthesis, compression and cooling favour ammonia. Industrial conditions balance equilibrium yield with reaction speed. A catalyst speeds the approach to equilibrium. Heating favours the endothermic direction; for this exothermic forward reaction, heating decreases K.

Compression in the Haber process

For N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃, Q = [NH₃]²/([N₂][H₂]³). Halving volume doubles every gas concentration instantly. The numerator increases fourfold; the denominator increases sixteenfold. Q becomes K/4, so the net reaction is forward. Although all concentrations initially rise, that instantaneous rise is different from the later reaction shift.

Compression factor f: Qnew/Qold = f^Δνgas
Δνgas = (gas product coefficients) − (gas reactant coefficients)

Partial pressure is the pressure an individual gas contributes: for an ideal mixture, pᵢ = nᵢRT/V = yᵢptotal, where yᵢ is its mole fraction. This ideal-gas result explains the “fewer gas moles” rule. An inert gas at constant volume leaves reacting partial pressures unchanged. At constant total pressure, adding inert gas expands the mixture and can favour the side with more gas moles.

Physical chemistry · follow the reasoning

Derive the effect of temperature on K

ΔᵣH° is standard reaction enthalpy, not an activation barrier. T₁ and T₂ are absolute temperatures.

  1. Start with equilibrium thermodynamics.
    ln K = −ΔᵣG°/(RT)
    ΔᵣG° changes with temperature too, so treating it as constant during differentiation would give the wrong general result.
  2. Use the Gibbs–Helmholtz relation. It collects that temperature dependence:
    d(ΔᵣG°/T)/dT = −ΔᵣH°/T²
    d(ln K)/dT = ΔᵣH°/(RT²)
    With ΔᵣH° < 0, increasing T decreases ln K and K.
  3. Integrate over a range where ΔᵣH° is nearly constant.
    ln(K₂/K₁) = (ΔᵣH°/R) ∫ from T₁ to T₂ T⁻² dT
    = (ΔᵣH°/R)(1/T₁ − 1/T₂)
    The algebra resembles Arrhenius, but this equation predicts equilibrium using reaction enthalpy; Arrhenius predicts a rate constant using activation energy.

Illustrative exothermic reaction: with ΔᵣH° = −40.0 kJ mol⁻¹, warming from 298 to 308 K gives ln(K₂/K₁) = −0.524, so K₂ = 0.592K₁. Faster reaction on heating can accompany a smaller equilibrium product ratio.

When this works: The differential relation uses ΔᵣH° at each temperature; the integrated expression assumes it is approximately constant over the interval.

3.1 Solutions, ions & net ionic equations

An ionic solid can dissociate into hydrated ions when it dissolves. A molecular solute may remain as molecules; some molecular substances, such as acids, react with water to produce ions. Mobile ions allow a solution to conduct electricity.

A saturated solution is in equilibrium with undissolved solute at a specified temperature. Dissolving and crystallizing occur at equal rates. Solubility is the amount that dissolves to form a saturated solution, and can be reported in mol L⁻¹ or g L⁻¹.

For precipitation reactions, use the solubility table to predict products. Write a balanced molecular equation, split soluble strong electrolytes into ions, then cancel spectator ions to obtain the net ionic equation. Do not split solids, liquids, gases, or weak electrolytes into ions.

Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s)
Tutor explanation 3.1Dissolving means competing interactions, not disappearing matter

Say to students“When an ionic crystal dissolves, its ions leave the lattice and become surrounded by water. They still exist; they are dispersed and hydrated.”

Why can water separate ions?

Water is polar: its oxygen end attracts cations and its hydrogen ends attract anions. Separating a lattice costs energy; hydration releases energy. Mixing also changes entropy. Their combined Gibbs-energy change, rather than lattice strength alone, determines the equilibrium extent of dissolution. A molecular substance such as sugar can dissolve as neutral molecules, so dissolving is not synonymous with producing ions.

Saturation and electrical conductivity

A saturated solution has the equilibrium dissolved composition at the specified temperature and conditions. Excess solid provides a visible phase with which it can equilibrate, but a solution can be exactly saturated without visible solid. If solid is present, dissolution and crystallization rates balance. Conductivity requires mobile charged species; a dissolved sugar solution does not conduct like an ionic solution of comparable concentration.

Why spectator ions cancel

AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq) represents Ag⁺, NO₃⁻, Na⁺, and Cl⁻ in water. Na⁺ and NO₃⁻ occur unchanged on both sides. Cancelling them leaves Ag⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl(s), conserving both atoms and total charge. Keep weak electrolytes intact because they are not almost entirely free ions.

University connection & limits

A supersaturated solution has more dissolved solute than its equilibrium value and may persist until nucleation occurs. In a net ionic equation, “spectator” means no net chemical conversion; those ions can still affect activity coefficients and conductivity.

Ask the studentDoes “no visible solid” prove a solution is unsaturated? Expected: no. It could be unsaturated, just saturated, or temporarily supersaturated.

3.2 Ksp & molar solubility

The solubility product, Ksp, describes an ionic solid’s dissolution equilibrium. The solid is omitted from the expression.

CaF₂(s) ⇌ Ca²⁺(aq) + 2F⁻(aq)
Ksp = [Ca²⁺][F⁻]²

If the molar solubility in initially ion-free water is s, the ion concentrations are s and 2s, so Ksp = s(2s)² = 4s³. The relation between s and ion concentrations depends on stoichiometry.

To convert molar solubility to mass concentration, multiply by the solid’s molar mass. A smaller Ksp does not always mean a smaller molar solubility when comparing salts with different ion ratios.

Tutor explanation 3.2Ksp is an ion-activity constraint; solubility is an amount

Say to students“Ksp fixes a product of free-ion activities. Molar solubility tells us how many moles of solid dissolved per litre. The balanced equation connects them.”

Derive the solubility equation before inserting numbers

For MₚXq(s) ⇌ pM + qX, dissolving s mol L⁻¹ into initially ion-free water gives [M] = ps and [X] = qs, provided neither ion has another important reaction.

Ksp ≈ (ps)ᵖ(qs)ᑫ = pᵖqᑫs^(p+q)
s ≈ [Ksp/(pᵖqᑫ)]^(1/(p+q))

For CaF₂ the ratio is 1:2, so Ksp ≈ s(2s)² = 4s³. For a 1:1 salt, Ksp ≈ s² instead. This is why two numerical Ksp values cannot by themselves rank molar solubilities across different formulas.

Convert the amount, not an individual ion concentration

mass solubility (g L⁻¹) = s (mol L⁻¹) × molar mass of solid (g mol⁻¹)

Use the formula-unit molar mass. In the MX₂ example, [X⁻] = 2s, but the dissolved amount of solid is s.

s is moles of formula units dissolved per litre; c° = 1 mol L⁻¹. M and X below label ions, not molarity.

    Ksp = aMᵖ aXᑫ ≈ (ps/c°)ᵖ(qs/c°)ᑫ

4.1 Acids, bases & conjugate pairs

A Brønsted–Lowry acid donates a proton, H⁺; a Brønsted–Lowry base accepts one. In water, H⁺ is commonly represented as H₃O⁺. A conjugate acid–base pair differs by exactly one proton and one unit of charge.

NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻

Here NH₃ is the base and NH₄⁺ its conjugate acid. H₂O is the acid and OH⁻ its conjugate base. An amphiprotic substance, such as HCO₃⁻, can donate or accept a proton.

Proton-transfer equilibria favour the side with the weaker acid and weaker base. Consult the supplied acid-strength table to identify the relevant species.

Tutor explanation 4.1Follow one proton, then explain why it transfers

Say to students“An acid and a base are roles in a reaction. Find the species that loses one H⁺ and the species that receives it.”

Charge is a useful check

Removing H⁺ leaves the electron pair behind, so the conjugate base has one fewer H and a charge one unit more negative. H₂CO₃ gives HCO₃⁻; NH₄⁺ gives NH₃. Adding H⁺ reverses this rule. NH₃ accepts H⁺ using its nitrogen lone pair. Water can accept a proton to become H₃O⁺ or donate one to become OH⁻: it is amphiprotic. HCO₃⁻ likewise has two possible roles.

Derive the weaker-acid/weaker-base rule

For HA + B ⇌ A⁻ + BH⁺, combine the ionization of HA with the reverse ionization of BH⁺. Their constants divide:

Ktransfer = Ka(HA)/Ka(BH⁺)
= 10^[pKa(BH⁺) − pKa(HA)]

A larger pKa for BH⁺ means it is the weaker acid, so proton transfer to B is favoured. This compares acids in the same solvent at the same temperature.

University connection & limits

H⁺(aq) is shorthand for a solvated proton; water forms a fluctuating hydrogen-bonded network, not a reservoir of bare protons. H₃O⁺ is the useful school representation. The Lewis definition is broader: a base donates an electron pair and an acid accepts one, so acid–base chemistry can occur without proton transfer.

4.2 Strength, concentration & water

Strong describes essentially complete ionization or dissociation under the stated aqueous conditions. Concentrated describes a large amount of solute per volume. A weak acid can be concentrated; a strong acid can be dilute.

2H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + OH⁻
Kw = [H₃O⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C

Neutral water has equal H₃O⁺ and OH⁻ concentrations at any temperature. At 25 °C these are 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ M. Because Kw changes with temperature, neutral pH is not always 7.

Common trap Do not assume every hydrogen atom in an acid formula ionizes completely. For example, the second ionization of sulfuric acid requires its own equilibrium treatment when relevant.
Tutor explanation 4.2Strength is equilibrium preference; concentration is how much was added

Say to students“Strong tells us how completely an acid reacts with water. Concentrated tells us how much acid is present per litre. Those are separate questions.”

Why water appears on both sides of acid–base chemistry

One water molecule can donate a proton to another. The autoionization equilibrium has a small product of hydronium and hydroxide activities. Treating water’s solvent activity as one gives the classroom equation:

Kw ≈ [H₃O⁺][OH⁻]
Neutral dilute water: [H₃O⁺] = [OH⁻] ≈ √Kw
pHneutral ≈ ½pKw

At 25 °C, √(10⁻¹⁴) = 10⁻⁷ M. At another temperature, neutrality still requires balanced acidic and basic ions, but the neutral pH changes with Kw. In dilute neutral pure water, equal concentrations and activities coincide to the approximation used here.

Why an acid formula does not give all its hydronium at once

Each proton loss has its own equilibrium constant. H₂SO₄ → HSO₄⁻ and HSO₄⁻ ⇌ SO₄²⁻ are different ionization steps. A proton already leaving a negatively charged species generally faces a less favourable equilibrium; use the assigned data rather than counting H atoms.

University connection & limits

Water levels acids that protonate it essentially completely: their aqueous hydronium concentrations mainly reflect analytical concentration. “Strong” is solvent dependent. Acid strength also reflects bond energies and stabilization/solvation of the conjugate base; it is not explained by bond polarity alone.

4.3 The logarithmic pH scale
pH = −log₁₀[H₃O⁺] · pOH = −log₁₀[OH⁻]
[H₃O⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ · pH + pOH = pKw
At 25 °C: pKw = 14.00

These classroom equations use molar concentration as an approximation to activity in dilute solution. Each pH unit corresponds to a tenfold change in hydronium concentration. Lower pH means greater hydronium concentration.

A pH outside 0–14 is possible; 0 and 14 are not absolute limits. Very concentrated solutions need activity corrections, while extremely dilute strong acids need water’s own ionization included.

For logarithms, the number of decimal places in pH corresponds to the number of significant figures in the concentration. Keep guard digits during calculations and round at the end.

Tutor explanation 4.3Derive every pH conversion from the logarithm definition

Say to students“The pH scale compresses powers of ten. A change of one unit changes hydronium activity by a factor of ten, rather than adding a fixed amount of acid.”

Why the minus sign and the inverse power?

For a hydronium activity of 10⁻³, log₁₀ is −3, so pH is +3. Applying the inverse operation gives a(H⁺) = 10⁻ᵖᴴ. In the dilute concentration model, insert the numerical molarity for activity.

pH = −log₁₀a(H⁺) ≈ −log₁₀([H₃O⁺]/c°)
pH + pOH = −log₁₀[a(H⁺)a(OH⁻)] = pKw
a(H⁺)₁/a(H⁺)₂ = 10^(pH₂ − pH₁)

Why pH decimal places track concentration significant figures

Writing 2.5 × 10⁻³ separates the power-of-ten scale from the measured coefficient. The logarithm’s fractional part carries that measurement precision: two significant figures become two decimal places in pH. Retain guard digits.

Why 10⁻⁸ M strong acid does not have pH 8

C is analytical HCl concentration; h = [H₃O⁺]; Kw uses the dilute concentration convention in this calculation.

  1. Conserve electric charge. Fully dissociated HCl gives [Cl⁻] = C. Since the bulk solution is electrically neutral:
    h = [Cl⁻] + [OH⁻] = C + [OH⁻]
    Water contributes hydronium as well as hydroxide; h is not simply C at extreme dilution.
  2. Use water equilibrium.
    [OH⁻] = Kw/h
    h = C + Kw/h → h² − Ch − Kw = 0
    h = [C + √(C² + 4Kw)]/2
    Reject the negative root because concentrations cannot be negative.
  3. Calculate, then take the logarithm. At 25 °C, C = 1.0 × 10⁻⁸ M and Kw = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ give h = 1.051 × 10⁻⁷ M, so pH ≈ 6.978.

4.4 Strong-acid/base mixtures & titration

For a mixture, do stoichiometry before pH. Calculate moles of acid and base, apply the balanced neutralization ratio, find the excess, and divide by total solution volume. Then calculate pH or pOH.

H₃O⁺ + OH⁻ → 2H₂O
n = cV, with V in litres

In a titration, a standard solution of known concentration reacts with the analyte. At the equivalence point, amounts match the balanced reaction’s stoichiometry. An indicator’s observed endpoint should be close to equivalence. They are different concepts.

To standardize a solution, titrate it against a suitable accurately known standard. Rinse the burette with titrant, remove tip bubbles, read the meniscus consistently, and use concordant titres. Follow your teacher’s lab-safety instructions.

Tutor explanation 4.4Conservation first, equilibrium second, logarithm last

Say to students“pH is a property of the final mixture. First count how many acid and base equivalents react; only then calculate the concentration left over.”

Where n = cV and dilution come from

The titrant is the solution delivered in measured volumes, usually from a burette; the analyte is the substance whose amount or concentration is being determined. Concentration is defined as c = n/V, so n = cV. A 25.0 mL sample of 0.100 mol L⁻¹ acid contains 0.00250 mol, not 2.50 mol. If no solute reacts or escapes during dilution, n before = n after, giving c₁V₁ = c₂V₂. Neutralization does change the species amounts, so dilution alone cannot solve an acid–base mixture.

Use the actual stoichiometric ratio

H₃O⁺ and OH⁻ react 1:1, but one formula unit may supply more than one equivalent. For HCl and fully dissolved Ca(OH)₂, n(HCl) = 2n(Ca(OH)₂) at equivalence. After finding any excess, divide by total mixed volume, assuming additive volumes. Do not average the two original pH values.

Equivalence, endpoint, and standardization

Equivalence is the stoichiometric condition. Endpoint is the observed colour or instrument signal chosen to locate it. Standardization determines a solution’s actual concentration from a known reacting amount; a suitable primary standard is sufficiently pure, stable, and accurately weighable. An air bubble that fills during delivery makes the burette reading overstate the liquid actually delivered to the flask.

University connection & limits

Neutralization has a large but finite constant: combining H₃O⁺ + OH⁻ → 2H₂O with water ionization gives K ≈ 1/Kw. Near equivalence or at extreme dilution, water equilibrium must still be respected. Weak-acid equivalence needs hydrolysis, not an automatic pH of 7.

5.1 Weak acids & Ka

A weak acid only partly transfers protons to water. Its equilibrium constant Ka quantifies its acid strength at a specified temperature.

HA + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + A⁻
Ka = [H₃O⁺][A⁻]/[HA]

For an initially pure weak acid of concentration C, let x be the amount ionized, neglecting water’s contribution when justified. Then [H₃O⁺] = [A⁻] = x and [HA] = C − x, giving Ka = x²/(C − x).

If x is small relative to C, x ≈ √(KaC). Check 100x/C ≤ 5% after solving. Otherwise use the quadratic. Percent ionization is 100x/C; dilution increases percent ionization even though hydronium concentration usually decreases.

5.2 Weak bases, conjugates & hydrolysis
B + H₂O ⇌ BH⁺ + OH⁻
Kb = [BH⁺][OH⁻]/[B]
Ka(BH⁺) × Kb(B) = Kw

For a weak base, solve for [OH⁻], then find pOH and pH. KaKb = Kw applies to a conjugate pair at the same temperature, not an arbitrary acid and base.

Hydrolysis is an ion’s acid–base reaction with water. Acetate produces some OH⁻ and makes a solution basic; ammonium produces some H₃O⁺ and makes it acidic. Ions from strong acids and strong bases, such as Na⁺ and Cl⁻, have negligible hydrolysis in the usual school model. Small, highly charged hydrated metal ions can acidify water.

For salts with both acidic and basic ions, compare the relevant Ka and Kb and account for competing equilibria if needed.

What salt hydrolysis means

Acetate reacts as CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃COOH + OH⁻, with Kb = Kw/Ka(acetic acid). Ammonium reacts as NH₄⁺ + H₂O ⇌ NH₃ + H₃O⁺, with Ka = Kw/Kb(ammonia). Dissociation of the salt happens first; hydrolysis is the subsequent reaction of an ion with water.

Physical chemistry · follow the reasoning

Why KaKb = Kw and what it predicts for a salt

HA and A⁻ must be a conjugate pair. The equilibrium constants below are thermodynamic activity constants.

  1. Add the acid and base reactions.
    HA + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + A⁻
    A⁻ + H₂O ⇌ HA + OH⁻
    Sum: 2H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + OH⁻
    HA and A⁻ cancel, leaving water autoionization.
  2. Multiply the constants. Taking solvent water’s activity as one:
    KaKb = [a(H₃O⁺)a(A⁻)/a(HA)]
    × [a(HA)a(OH⁻)/a(A⁻)]
    = a(H₃O⁺)a(OH⁻) = Kw
    Taking −log₁₀ gives pKa + pKb = pKw.
  3. Use the actual salt ion. A 0.100 M acetate solution with Ka(acetic acid) = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ has Kb(acetate) = 10⁻¹⁴/(1.8 × 10⁻⁵) = 5.56 × 10⁻¹⁰ at 25 °C. In the dilute concentration model, [OH⁻] ≈ √(KbC) = 7.45 × 10⁻⁶ M; pOH ≈ 5.128 and pH ≈ 8.872.

Check: the hydrolyzed fraction is only 0.00745%, and the calculated hydroxide is well above 10⁻⁷ M. Both the small-change and negligible-water assumptions are reasonable within this model.

When this works: The conjugate species, solvent, temperature, and standard states match. The numerical pH uses the usual ideal classroom approximation.

5.3 Buffers & indicators

A buffer contains appreciable amounts of a weak acid and its conjugate base, or a weak base and its conjugate acid. The base member consumes added acid; the acid member consumes added base.

pH ≈ pKa + log₁₀([A⁻]/[HA])
pKa = −log₁₀Ka

This Henderson–Hasselbalch form is useful when both buffer components remain appreciable and concentration ratios approximate activity ratios. A buffer works best around pH = pKa, typically within about one pH unit. Greater amounts of its components provide greater capacity.

After adding a strong acid or base, first update the buffer component moles by neutralization, then use the new ratio. If one component is exhausted, the buffer equation is no longer suitable.

An indicator is a weak acid/base whose forms have different colours. Choose one whose transition range lies within the steep pH change near the expected equivalence point.

Physical chemistry · follow the reasoning

Follow every algebra step in the buffer equation

pH = −log₁₀ a(H₃O⁺); pKa = −log₁₀ Ka. HA and A⁻ are the two buffer partners.

  1. Isolate hydrogen-ion activity.
    Ka = a(H₃O⁺)a(A⁻)/a(HA)
    a(H₃O⁺) = Ka × a(HA)/a(A⁻)
  2. Take the negative base-ten logarithm.
    pH = −log₁₀ Ka − log₁₀[a(HA)/a(A⁻)]
    = pKa + log₁₀[a(A⁻)/a(HA)]
    ≈ pKa + log₁₀([A⁻]/[HA])
    The plus sign comes from inverting the ratio. More A⁻ therefore raises pH.
  3. React added strong acid first. Start with 0.100 mol HA and 0.100 mol A⁻; add 0.0100 mol HCl. H₃O⁺ + A⁻ → HA + H₂O leaves 0.0900 mol A⁻ and 0.110 mol HA.
    pH ≈ 4.76 + log₁₀(0.0900/0.110) = 4.67
    The common final volume cancels from the concentration ratio.

Capacity matters: a buffer containing only 0.00100 mol of each partner begins at about the same pH, but adding 0.0100 mol HCl exhausts its A⁻ reserve. Henderson–Hasselbalch cannot then be used with a negative “remaining amount.”

When this works: The activity relation follows directly from acid equilibrium. Replacing activities by prepared or post-reaction amounts requires ideal behaviour and equilibrium changes small relative to both buffer reserves.

5.4 Titration curves, anhydrides & acid rain

Strong-acid/strong-base equivalence is approximately pH 7 at 25 °C. Weak-acid/strong-base equivalence is basic because the conjugate base hydrolyzes. Weak-base/strong-acid equivalence is acidic.

At half-equivalence in a weak-acid/strong-base titration, equal amounts of HA and A⁻ remain, so pH ≈ pKa. Before equivalence, use buffer chemistry when applicable; at equivalence, use hydrolysis; beyond equivalence, use excess strong titrant.

Many nonmetal oxides are acidic anhydrides: they form acids when reacting with water. Some metal oxides are basic anhydrides. Sulfur and nitrogen oxides contribute to acid rain through atmospheric reactions. Test an oxide’s behaviour using its actual reaction; these patterns have exceptions.

Common trap “Equivalence” means stoichiometric amounts have reacted. It does not mean the final solution must be neutral.

Tutor explanation 5.4Read a titration curve by asking which species controls pH

Say to students“The curve is several chemical situations joined together. At each stage, identify the major species before choosing an equation.”

Weak-acid/strong-base titration in four regions

  1. Initially: mostly HA; solve its weak-acid equilibrium.
  2. Before equivalence: neutralization creates A⁻ while HA remains. Use their post-reaction ratio when both are appreciable.
  3. At equivalence: HA has been converted mainly to A⁻. Its analytical concentration is initial acid moles divided by total volume. Use Kb = Kw/Ka to calculate hydrolysis and hydroxide.
  4. Beyond equivalence: excess strong base usually controls [OH⁻]; divide excess moles by total volume.

At half-equivalence, half the original HA has become A⁻. Their analytical amounts are equal, so pH ≈ pKa in the buffer approximation. Equivalence concerns moles, not equality of original volumes or a mandatory pH of 7. A weak-base/strong-acid titration has the corresponding acidic conjugate species at equivalence.

Make anhydrides and acid rain chemical, rather than a slogan

An acid anhydride forms an acid on reaction with water: SO₃ + H₂O → H₂SO₄. A basic example is CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂. Acid rain involves oxidation and hydration of atmospheric sulfur and nitrogen species; it is not simply every gas dissolving as a strong acid. Ordinary rain is already mildly acidic because CO₂ participates in carbonate chemistry.

6.1 Oxidation, reduction & oxidation numbers

Oxidation is loss of electrons; reduction is gain of electrons. Both happen together. An oxidizing agent accepts electrons and is reduced; a reducing agent donates electrons and is oxidized.

An element in its elemental form has oxidation number 0. A monatomic ion’s number equals its charge. Oxidation numbers sum to the total charge of a species. Oxygen is usually −2, except in cases such as peroxides; hydrogen is usually +1, except in metal hydrides.

Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ (oxidation)
Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu (reduction)

An increase in oxidation number signals oxidation. Here Zn is the reducing agent, and Cu²⁺ is the oxidizing agent.

Tutor explanation 6.1Oxidation numbers are an electron-accounting convention

Say to students“The oxidizing agent causes another species to lose electrons by accepting them. The agent itself is therefore reduced.”

Why oxidation number rises when electrons are lost

In Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻, zinc’s assigned number changes from 0 to +2, consistent with losing two negatively charged electrons. Cu²⁺ → Cu changes +2 to 0 by gaining two. An oxidation number is the formal charge assigned if bonding electrons are given to the more electronegative atom. In covalent molecules it need not equal a measured atomic charge or a Lewis formal charge.

Use the charge sum to determine unknown numbers

For MnO₄⁻, let Mn be x. Then x + 4(−2) = −1, so x = +7. Reducing it to Mn²⁺ changes Mn by five units and requires five electrons per Mn. Hydrogen is −1 in ionic metal hydrides such as NaH; oxygen is −1 in peroxides such as H₂O₂. These exceptions follow the electron-assignment rules, rather than breaking charge conservation.

Oxidation and reduction must be coupled

The electrons lost and gained cancel in the overall balanced reaction. In covalent redox reactions, changed oxidation numbers track electron redistribution even when no free electrons are observed in solution.

University connection & limits

Electronegativity describes an atom’s tendency to attract bonding electrons. Formal charge instead shares bonding electrons equally. Distinguishing the two accounting schemes helps explain why carbon can have oxidation number +4 in CO₂ while a usual Lewis structure assigns carbon formal charge zero.

Ask the studentIn 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO, which is the oxidizing agent? Expected: O₂, because its oxygen atoms go from 0 to −2 and gain electron density.

6.2 Balance redox reactions

Separate the oxidation and reduction half-reactions. In acidic solution:

  1. Balance all atoms except O and H.
  2. Balance oxygen using H₂O, then hydrogen using H⁺.
  3. Balance charge using electrons.
  4. Multiply half-reactions to equalize electron transfer; add and cancel.

For basic solution, balance as if acidic, then add enough OH⁻ to both sides to convert every H⁺ into H₂O. Cancel excess water. Check every element and total charge.

In redox titrations, use the balanced equation to convert titrant moles to analyte moles. Electron transfer determines the mole ratio; it is not automatically 1:1.

Tutor explanation 6.2The half-reaction method enforces both atom and charge conservation

Say to students“Water supplies missing oxygen atoms, H⁺ balances hydrogen in acid, and electrons balance charge. Each addition has a specific job.”

Walk through an acidic half-reaction

Start MnO₄⁻ → Mn²⁺. Add 4H₂O on the right for oxygen, then 8H⁺ on the left for hydrogen. The left charge is now +7 and the right charge +2. Adding five electrons to the left makes both +2:

MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O

Pair this with 5Fe²⁺ → 5Fe³⁺ + 5e⁻. Adding cancels electrons and gives a 1:5 permanganate-to-iron ratio for an acidic redox titration. Use n = cV, then this ratio, before converting to analyte mass or concentration.

Why the basic-solution conversion works

Adding the same number of OH⁻ to both sides preserves the equation. Each H⁺ + OH⁻ becomes H₂O; cancelling common water leaves a balance appropriate to basic notation. Check atoms and total charge again. This procedure balances specified reactants and products; it does not select which products are stable in that medium.

University connection & limits

Permanganate often gives different manganese products in different conditions. If MnO₂ is the specified basic-medium product, the balanced half-reaction is MnO₄⁻ + 2H₂O + 3e⁻ → MnO₂(s) + 4OH⁻. Do not merely keep the acidic product Mn²⁺ because an algebraic conversion is possible. Electrons are accounting terms that disappear from the complete chemical reaction.

Ask the studentWhy are five Fe²⁺ needed per MnO₄⁻ for reduction to Mn²⁺ in acid? Expected: each Fe²⁺ loses one electron while each Mn accepts five.

6.3 Cells, standard potentials & spontaneity

A galvanic cell uses a spontaneous redox reaction to produce electrical energy. Oxidation occurs at the anode and reduction at the cathode. Electrons move through the external circuit from anode to cathode. The salt bridge maintains electrical neutrality by ion migration, rather than carrying electrons.

E°cell = E°red(cathode) − E°red(anode)
ΔG° = −nFE°cell

Use the tabulated reduction potentials in the subtraction formula. A positive E°cell indicates thermodynamic favourability for the reaction as written under standard conditions. It does not guarantee a fast reaction. Standard aqueous conditions use unit activity, often approximated by 1 M, and standard gas pressure is 1 bar; specify temperature, commonly 25 °C.

Multiplying a half-reaction does not multiply its electrode potential. For competing reactions, compare feasible oxidation and reduction pairs; actual conditions, kinetics, and overpotential can affect what is observed.

Follow the derivation: Derive Nernst from the same equilibrium energy equation →

Tutor explanation 6.3Voltage is energy per charge, so it is never multiplied like an amount

Say to students“The balanced equation counts how much charge moves. The cell voltage tells us how much energy can be obtained per unit charge.”

Explain the electrode rules with the zinc–copper cell

Zinc oxidation makes electrons available at the anode; copper-ion reduction consumes them at the cathode. The external wire carries electrons. In the salt bridge, anions generally migrate toward the zinc compartment as Zn²⁺ forms, and cations toward the copper compartment as Cu²⁺ is consumed. This prevents charge buildup that would stop the reaction.

Use reduction potentials consistently

Tabulated half-cell values are reductions relative to the standard hydrogen electrode, assigned zero. Reversing the anode reduction gives E°cell = E°red,cathode − E°red,anode. For zinc–copper, n = 2 and E° = 1.10 V, so ΔᵣG° ≈ −212 kJ per mole of reaction as written. The derivation below connects this energy to voltage and equilibrium.

Physical chemistry · follow the reasoning

Derive Nernst from the same equilibrium energy equation

n is electrons transferred per balanced cell reaction (dimensionless here); F = 96485 C mol⁻¹. E is reversible cell voltage.

  1. Convert chemical driving force to energy per charge. One mole of cell reaction transfers nF coulombs. The maximum electrical work delivered is nFE per mole of reaction:
    ΔᵣG = −nFE; ΔᵣG° = −nFE°
    The minus sign makes a favourable ΔᵣG < 0 correspond to positive E.
  2. Substitute into the Gibbs-energy equation.
    ΔᵣG = ΔᵣG° + RT ln Q
    −nFE = −nFE° + RT ln Q
    E = E° − (RT/nF) ln Q
    At 298.15 K, ln Q = 2.303 log₁₀Q, giving E = E° − (0.05916 V/n) log₁₀Q.
  3. Use the balanced zinc–copper reaction.
    Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s)
    n = 2; Q = a(Zn²⁺)/a(Cu²⁺)
    If Q = 100: E = 1.10 − (0.05916/2)log₁₀(100)
    ≈ 1.04 V
    Changing composition changes actual voltage without changing E° at fixed T.
  4. At equilibrium, put Q = K and E = 0.
    ln K = nFE°/(RT)
    At 298.15 K: log₁₀ K = nE°/(0.05916 V)
    Using the illustrative rounded E° = 1.10 V gives K ≈ 1.5 × 10³⁷. This predicts a strongly product-favoured equilibrium, not a particular current.

Scaling check: doubling the cell reaction doubles n and squares Q. The term (RT/2nF)ln(Q²) equals (RT/nF)ln Q, so E is unchanged.

When this works: Reversible thermodynamic cell voltage with consistent activities. Voltage measured while current flows can differ because of resistance, mass transport, and electrode kinetics. The 0.05916 V coefficient is specific to 25 °C.

Adapted from Atkins & de Paula, Physical Chemistry, 8th ed., §7.7, eqs. 7.27–7.30. Read printed p. 220–221 (PDF page 252) ↗

University connection & limits

Standard state is unit activity for dissolved species, pure phases, and standard gas pressure 1 bar; it does not by itself prescribe 25 °C. At nonstandard composition, E = E° − (RT/nF)ln Q, the Nernst equation. Use dimensionless Q. A positive actual E indicates a favourable forward cell reaction; positive E° refers to standard conditions. At equilibrium E = 0. Neither sign guarantees a fast reaction or a large current.

6.4 Electrolysis, plating & corrosion

An electrolytic cell uses an external power supply to drive a nonspontaneous reaction. Oxidation is still at the anode and reduction at the cathode, but the anode is positive and cathode negative, opposite to a galvanic cell’s signs.

In metal electroplating, the object is the cathode, where dissolved metal ions gain electrons and deposit. In aqueous electrolysis, consider water as a possible reactant alongside the ions.

Q = It · n(e⁻) = Q/F
F ≈ 96 485 C mol⁻¹ e⁻
n(metal) = n(e⁻)/z

Here I is current in amperes, t is time in seconds, and z is electrons per deposited metal atom. This ideal calculation assumes 100% current efficiency.

Iron corrosion couples metal oxidation with a reduction reaction, often involving oxygen and water. Coatings block exposure; sacrificial protection uses a more readily oxidized metal, such as zinc, to protect the iron.

Follow the derivation: Carry the units from current to plated mass →

Tutor explanation 6.4Derive plating mass from charge and explain corrosion as a local cell

Say to students“A current is a measured flow of charge. Count that charge, convert it to moles of electrons, and let the half-reaction tell you how much metal those electrons can produce.”

Why electrode signs reverse in electrolysis

The power supply withdraws electrons from the anode and pushes them toward the cathode. Oxidation still occurs at the anode and reduction at the cathode. The plated object must therefore be the cathode. In water, hydrogen or oxygen formation can compete with deposition or ion discharge.

Physical chemistry · follow the reasoning

Carry the units from current to plated mass

I is constant current, t is time, z is electron stoichiometry, and Mmetal is molar mass. Qcharge is electric charge, distinct from reaction quotient Q.

  1. Integrate the current.
    Qcharge = It
    (C s⁻¹) × s = C
    For varying current, replace It by the area ∫I dt.
  2. Count electrons, then metal atoms.
    n(e⁻) = Qcharge/F
    Mᶻ⁺ + ze⁻ → M → n(metal) = Qcharge/(zF)
    m = ηItMmetal/(zF)
    η is the fraction of charge that actually produces the desired deposit.
  3. Work an example. Copper plating at 2.00 A for 30.0 min uses t = 1800 s, z = 2, and MCu = 63.55 g mol⁻¹:
    Qcharge = 3600 C
    n(e⁻) = 3600/96485 = 0.0373 mol
    n(Cu) = 0.0373/2 = 0.0187 mol
    m(Cu) = 1.19 g at η = 1; 1.07 g at η = 0.900

Units check: C × (g mol⁻¹)/(C mol⁻¹) = g. Using minutes without conversion gives a mass 60 times too small.

When this works: The balanced deposition half-reaction is correct, with a known current efficiency. Charge can also drive competing reactions; applied voltage alone does not determine deposited mass.

Original charge-counting application of Atkins & de Paula, Physical Chemistry, 8th ed., §7.7, Faraday constant and charge per reaction extent. Read printed p. 220 (PDF page 252) ↗

University connection & limits

Corrosion can couple small anodic and cathodic regions on the same metal. A typical pair is Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻ and O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻; later reactions form rust. Connected zinc can oxidize sacrificially and protect iron while sufficient zinc and electrical contact remain. Observed electrolysis also depends on kinetics, overpotential, and resistance, so thermodynamic minimum voltage alone does not set the operating voltage.

Ask the studentFor the same charge, which deposits more moles: Ag⁺ → Ag or Cu²⁺ → Cu? Expected: silver, twice as many moles at 100% efficiency. Comparing masses additionally requires their molar masses.
