SOLUBILITY EQUILIBRIUM · A THERMODYNAMIC DERIVATION

Why does Ksp
have this form?

The second law gives the equilibrium condition. Mixing explains the concentration dependence. Together, they lead to the familiar solubility product.

The question that starts this lesson

“In tutorial.html, how is Ksp = [M]a[X]b derived from thermodynamic law?”

MXaXXb(s)⇌a M(aq)+b X(aq)\ce{M_{a}X_{b}(s) <=> aM(aq) + bX(aq)}MXa​XXb​(s)​aM(aq)+bX(aq)
Ksp=αM,eqaαX,eqb≈([M]eqc∘)a([X]eqc∘)bK_{sp}=\alpha_{M,\mathrm{eq}}^{a}\alpha_{X,\mathrm{eq}}^{b}\approx\left(\frac{[M]_{\mathrm{eq}}}{c^\circ}\right)^a\left(\frac{[X]_{\mathrm{eq}}}{c^\circ}\right)^bKsp​=αM,eqa​αX,eqb​≈(c∘[M]eq​​)a(c∘[X]eq​​)b

Coefficients a and b become exponents. Ion charges are omitted in this generic equation; an actual dissolution equation must balance charge.

Read 01–06 for the derivation. Sections 07–09 unpack the follow-up question: “What is mixing thermodynamics?” All equations are available offline.

01Start with the equilibrium condition

The first law accounts for energy transfers. The second law determines the direction in which a process can proceed spontaneously: the total entropy of the system and its surroundings increases, reaching a maximum at equilibrium under the appropriate constraints.

For a closed system at constant temperature and pressure, exchanging heat with a reservoir at the same temperature and doing only pressure–volume work, combine the two laws using Gibbs free energy:

G=H−TS,ΔSuniverse=ΔSsystem−ΔHsystemT=−ΔGsystemTG=H-TS,\qquad \Delta S_{\mathrm{universe}}=\Delta S_{\mathrm{system}}-\frac{\Delta H_{\mathrm{system}}}{T}=-\frac{\Delta G_{\mathrm{system}}}{T}G=H−TS,ΔSuniverse​=ΔSsystem​−TΔHsystem​​=−TΔGsystem​​

Spontaneous change therefore lowers the system’s Gibbs free energy. At fixed temperature and pressure, equilibrium is the minimum of G with respect to the allowed composition changes.

At dissolution equilibrium, with solid and solution both present

ΔrG=(∂G∂ξ)T,P=0\boxed{\Delta_rG=\left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial\xi}\right)_{T,P}=0}Δr​G=(∂ξ∂G​)T,P​=0​

The reaction extent ξ\xiξ measures how much of the written reaction has occurred. ΔrG\Delta_rGΔr​G is the slope of G against reaction extent: the Gibbs-energy change per mole of reaction at the current composition.

Keep these two quantities distinct. At equilibrium, ΔrG=0\Delta_rG=0Δr​G=0. The standard reaction Gibbs energy ΔrG∘\Delta_rG^\circΔr​G∘ usually is not zero; it helps determine where the equilibrium lies.

02Give each species a chemical potential

A species’ chemical potential is its partial molar Gibbs energy: how much G changes when a small amount of that species is added while temperature, pressure and the amounts of the other species are fixed.

μi=(∂G∂ni)T,P,nj≠i=μi∘+RTln⁡αi\mu_i=\left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial n_i}\right)_{T,P,n_{j\ne i}}=\mu_i^\circ+RT\ln\alpha_iμi​=(∂ni​∂G​)T,P,nj=i​​=μi∘​+RTlnαi​

Activity αi\alpha_iαi​ is a dimensionless effective concentration. It captures how a species’ chemical potential changes with composition. The logarithmic form is connected to mixing; the ideal-mixture derivation appears in section 08.

A lower activity gives a more negative logarithmic contribution. Adding an ion to a dilute solution therefore has a different Gibbs-energy cost from adding it to a concentrated solution.

03Let stoichiometry produce the powers

For one mole of the generic salt dissolving, a moles of M and b moles of X appear, while one mole of solid disappears. Add the product contributions and subtract the reactant contribution:

ΔrG=aμM+bμX−μsolid\Delta_rG=a\mu_M+b\mu_X-\mu_{\mathrm{solid}}Δr​G=aμM​+bμX​−μsolid​

Substitute the activity expression for each chemical potential. The standard-state terms form ΔrG∘=aμM∘+bμX∘−μsolid∘\Delta_rG^\circ=a\mu_M^\circ+b\mu_X^\circ-\mu_{\mathrm{solid}}^\circΔr​G∘=aμM∘​+bμX∘​−μsolid∘​:

ΔrG=ΔrG∘+RT(aln⁡αM+bln⁡αX−ln⁡αsolid)=ΔrG∘+RTln⁡(αMaαXbαsolid).\begin{aligned}\Delta_rG&=\Delta_rG^\circ+RT\left(a\ln\alpha_M+b\ln\alpha_X-\ln\alpha_{\mathrm{solid}}\right)\\&=\Delta_rG^\circ+RT\ln\left(\frac{\alpha_M^a\alpha_X^b}{\alpha_{\mathrm{solid}}}\right).\end{aligned}Δr​G​=Δr​G∘+RT(alnαM​+blnαX​−lnαsolid​)=Δr​G∘+RTln(αsolid​αMa​αXb​​).​

The activity of the pure solid is one in the usual pure-phase convention, so its logarithmic contribution vanishes. Define the dissolution reaction quotient Q using the current ion activities:

Q=αMaαXb,ΔrG=ΔrG∘+RTln⁡QQ=\alpha_M^a\alpha_X^b,\qquad \boxed{\Delta_rG=\Delta_rG^\circ+RT\ln Q}Q=αMa​αXb​,Δr​G=Δr​G∘+RTlnQ​

The exponents follow directly from the logarithm identity:

aln⁡x+bln⁡y=ln⁡(xayb)a\ln x+b\ln y=\ln(x^ay^b)alnx+blny=ln(xayb)

Why the solid disappears. Its activity is one, not zero. Adding more of the same pure solid does not change its chemical potential at fixed temperature and pressure. The equilibrium condition requires some solid to remain; an unsaturated solution need not satisfy Q = Ksp.

04Set the reaction driving force to zero

At equilibrium, use ΔrG=0\Delta_rG=0Δr​G=0 and evaluate Q using the equilibrium composition:

0=ΔrG∘+RTln⁡(αM,eqaαX,eqb)0=\Delta_rG^\circ+RT\ln\left(\alpha_{M,\mathrm{eq}}^a\alpha_{X,\mathrm{eq}}^b\right)0=Δr​G∘+RTln(αM,eqa​αX,eqb​)
ln⁡(αM,eqaαX,eqb)=−ΔrG∘RT\ln\left(\alpha_{M,\mathrm{eq}}^a\alpha_{X,\mathrm{eq}}^b\right)=-\frac{\Delta_rG^\circ}{RT}ln(αM,eqa​αX,eqb​)=−RTΔr​G∘​

Exponentiate both sides. At a specified temperature, pressure and standard-state convention, the right-hand side is fixed, so define it as the solubility product constant:

Ksp=αM,eqaαX,eqb=exp⁡(−ΔrG∘RT)\boxed{K_{sp}=\alpha_{M,\mathrm{eq}}^a\alpha_{X,\mathrm{eq}}^b=\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta_rG^\circ}{RT}\right)}Ksp​=αM,eqa​αX,eqb​=exp(−RTΔr​G∘​)​

The standard Gibbs energy combines the standard enthalpy and entropy of dissolution:

ΔrG∘=ΔrH∘−TΔrS∘,ln⁡Ksp=−ΔrH∘RT+ΔrS∘R\Delta_rG^\circ=\Delta_rH^\circ-T\Delta_rS^\circ,\qquad \ln K_{sp}=-\frac{\Delta_rH^\circ}{RT}+\frac{\Delta_rS^\circ}{R}Δr​G∘=Δr​H∘−TΔr​S∘,lnKsp​=−RTΔr​H∘​+RΔr​S∘​

Lattice disruption, solvent interactions and entropy all contribute to where equilibrium lies. The first law alone does not determine the product expression or the equilibrium composition.

05Recover the tutorial’s concentration formula

For a dissolved ion, a concentration-based activity can be written using an activity coefficient γi\gamma_iγi​:

αi=γi[i]c∘\alpha_i=\gamma_i\frac{[i]}{c^\circ}αi​=γi​c∘[i]​

In the dilute ideal approximation, γi≈1\gamma_i\approx1γi​≈1. Substitute this into the thermodynamic expression:

Ksp≈([M]eqc∘)a([X]eqc∘)bK_{sp}\approx\left(\frac{[M]_{\mathrm{eq}}}{c^\circ}\right)^a\left(\frac{[X]_{\mathrm{eq}}}{c^\circ}\right)^bKsp​≈(c∘[M]eq​​)a(c∘[X]eq​​)b

When concentrations are entered numerically in mol L⁻¹ and c∘=1 mol L−1c^\circ=1\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}c∘=1 molL−1, this becomes the familiar school shorthand:

Ksp=[M]eqa[X]eqb(school concentration convention)\boxed{K_{sp}=[M]_{\mathrm{eq}}^a[X]_{\mathrm{eq}}^b\quad\text{(school concentration convention)}}Ksp​=[M]eqa​[X]eqb​(school concentration convention)​

The rigorous thermodynamic Ksp is dimensionless. A concentration product written without the standard-concentration factors is an approximate convention. At appreciable ionic strength, activity coefficients matter and the simple concentration product need not remain constant.

Use free-ion concentrations. Metal tied up in a complex, or an anion converted into another acid–base form, cannot simply be counted as free M or X.

06Work through calcium fluoride

CaFX2(s)⇌CaX2+(aq)+2 FX−(aq)\ce{CaF2(s) <=> Ca^2+(aq) + 2F-(aq)}CaFX2​(s)​CaX2+(aq)+2FX−(aq)

The coefficient of fluoride is two, so its chemical-potential contribution is doubled. That multiplier becomes an exponent:

ΔrG=μCaX2++2μFX−−μCaFX2(s)\Delta_rG=\mu_{\ce{Ca^2+}}+2\mu_{\ce{F-}}-\mu_{\ce{CaF2(s)}}Δr​G=μCaX2+​+2μFX−​−μCaFX2​(s)​
Ksp=αCaX2+αFX−2≈([CaX2+]c∘)([FX−]c∘)2K_{sp}=\alpha_{\ce{Ca^2+}}\alpha_{\ce{F-}}^2\approx\left(\frac{[\ce{Ca^2+}]}{c^\circ}\right)\left(\frac{[\ce{F-}]}{c^\circ}\right)^2Ksp​=αCaX2+​αFX−2​≈(c∘[CaX2+]​)(c∘[FX−]​)2

The exponent follows the number of ions produced, not the magnitude of the ion’s charge. Calcium has charge +2 but exponent one; fluoride has charge −1 but exponent two.

Use a hypothetical model value Ksp=4.0×10−11K_{sp}=4.0\times10^{-11}Ksp​=4.0×10−11. Assume initially ion-free water, dilute ideal behaviour and no important side reactions. If the molar solubility is s:

A common ion changes the relation to s. With initial fluoride concentration C, the school expression becomes Ksp≈s(C+2s)2K_{sp}\approx s(C+2s)^2Ksp​≈s(C+2s)2 using numerical molar concentrations. The thermodynamic constant remains fixed at the same temperature; the equilibrium solubility changes.

07What does “mixing thermodynamics” mean?

Mixing thermodynamics describes how mixing changes entropy, enthalpy and Gibbs free energy. Imagine two ideal gases separated by a partition. Once it is removed, each species can occupy the whole container. The mixed state has many more possible microscopic arrangements.

More accessible arrangements mean higher entropy. For an ideal mixture at the same temperature, mixing has no enthalpy change, so its positive entropy change lowers Gibbs free energy:

ΔHmix=0,ΔSmix>0,ΔGmix=−TΔSmix<0\Delta H_{\mathrm{mix}}=0,\qquad\Delta S_{\mathrm{mix}}>0,\qquad\Delta G_{\mathrm{mix}}=-T\Delta S_{\mathrm{mix}}<0ΔHmix​=0,ΔSmix​>0,ΔGmix​=−TΔSmix​<0

Dissolved ions spreading among solvent molecules also have a mixing contribution. Salt dissolution additionally involves a crystal lattice and ion–solvent interactions. Ideal mixing is the model that reveals the logarithmic term; it is not the entire energy balance for dissolving a real solid.

08See why mixing produces a logarithm

For two ideal components, let nA,nBn_A,n_BnA​,nB​ be their amounts and xA=nA/(nA+nB)x_A=n_A/(n_A+n_B)xA​=nA​/(nA​+nB​), xB=nB/(nA+nB)x_B=n_B/(n_A+n_B)xB​=nB​/(nA​+nB​) their mole fractions. The entropy of mixing is:

ΔSmix=−R(nAln⁡xA+nBln⁡xB)\Delta S_{\mathrm{mix}}=-R\left(n_A\ln x_A+n_B\ln x_B\right)ΔSmix​=−R(nA​lnxA​+nB​lnxB​)

For a genuine mixture, each mole fraction lies between zero and one. Its logarithm is negative, so the minus sign makes the entropy change positive. With zero ideal mixing enthalpy:

ΔGmix=RT(nAln⁡xA+nBln⁡xB)\Delta G_{\mathrm{mix}}=RT\left(n_A\ln x_A+n_B\ln x_B\right)ΔGmix​=RT(nA​lnxA​+nB​lnxB​)

Add this contribution to the pure-component Gibbs energies, denoted by a star:

G=nAμA∗+nBμB∗+RT(nAln⁡xA+nBln⁡xB)G=n_A\mu_A^*+n_B\mu_B^*+RT\left(n_A\ln x_A+n_B\ln x_B\right)G=nA​μA∗​+nB​μB∗​+RT(nA​lnxA​+nB​lnxB​)

Now differentiate with respect to the amount of A. Remember that adding A changes both mole fractions. The result is:

μA=μA∗+RTln⁡xA\boxed{\mu_A=\mu_A^*+RT\ln x_A}μA​=μA∗​+RTlnxA​​
Show the derivative, including the cancellation

Let n=nA+nBn=n_A+n_Bn=nA​+nB​. Rewrite the mixing term first:

ΔGmixRT=nAln⁡nA+nBln⁡nB−nln⁡n\frac{\Delta G_{\mathrm{mix}}}{RT}=n_A\ln n_A+n_B\ln n_B-n\ln nRTΔGmix​​=nA​lnnA​+nB​lnnB​−nlnn

Holding nBn_BnB​ fixed gives:

∂(ΔGmix)∂nA=RT[(ln⁡nA+1)−(ln⁡n+1)]=RTln⁡nAn=RTln⁡xA\frac{\partial(\Delta G_{\mathrm{mix}})}{\partial n_A}=RT\left[(\ln n_A+1)-(\ln n+1)\right]=RT\ln\frac{n_A}{n}=RT\ln x_A∂nA​∂(ΔGmix​)​=RT[(lnnA​+1)−(lnn+1)]=RTlnnnA​​=RTlnxA​

The +1 terms cancel. The pure-component term adds μA∗\mu_A^*μA∗​, yielding the chemical potential above. Intermediate amount logarithms are understood with a common reference unit; the final logarithm is a dimensionless ratio.

Mix one mole of each ideal component

For nA=nB=1.00 moln_A=n_B=1.00\ \mathrm{mol}nA​=nB​=1.00 mol, the mole fractions are both 0.500:

ΔSmix=−R[1.00ln⁡0.500+1.00ln⁡0.500]=2Rln⁡2≈11.5 J K−1\Delta S_{\mathrm{mix}}=-R\left[1.00\ln0.500+1.00\ln0.500\right]=2R\ln2\approx11.5\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}ΔSmix​=−R[1.00ln0.500+1.00ln0.500]=2Rln2≈11.5 JK−1
ΔGmix=−(298 K)(2Rln⁡2)≈−3.43 kJ\Delta G_{\mathrm{mix}}=-(298\ \mathrm{K})(2R\ln2)\approx-3.43\ \mathrm{kJ}ΔGmix​=−(298 K)(2Rln2)≈−3.43 kJ

Check the sign and units: entropy increases; multiplying by −T gives a negative energy change. Mixing remains favourable in this ideal model.

For a dilute solute, mole fraction is proportional to concentration when the solvent dominates. Absorbing the fixed reference factor into a solute standard chemical potential gives the concentration-based form:

μi≈μi∘+RTln⁡([i]c∘)\mu_i\approx\mu_i^\circ+RT\ln\left(\frac{[i]}{c^\circ}\right)μi​≈μi∘​+RTln(c∘[i]​)

Real ions interact, so use activities for the general expression μi=μi∘+RTln⁡αi\mu_i=\mu_i^\circ+RT\ln\alpha_iμi​=μi∘​+RTlnαi​. The second law supplies the equilibrium condition; the mixing model, together with the activity framework, supplies the composition dependence.

09Watch the driving force change with concentration

Combine ΔrG∘=−RTln⁡Ksp\Delta_rG^\circ=-RT\ln K_{sp}Δr​G∘=−RTlnKsp​ with the current-composition expression. This makes the physical meaning especially clear:

ΔrG=RTln⁡(QKsp)\boxed{\Delta_rG=RT\ln\left(\frac{Q}{K_{sp}}\right)}Δr​G=RTln(Ksp​Q​)​

As dissolution raises the free-ion activities, Q rises and the logarithmic term becomes larger. With enough solid available, the solution reaches saturation at Q = Ksp. If all solid dissolves first, the final solution can remain unsaturated.

At equilibrium there is no net dissolution: dissolution and crystallization can continue at equal rates. Thermodynamics predicts the direction and equilibrium composition; kinetic barriers determine how quickly they are reached. A supersaturated solution can persist if crystallization is slow.

The concentration product is the mathematical result of adding the ions’ chemical potentials and requiring zero reaction Gibbs-energy change at equilibrium.
